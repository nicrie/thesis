\chapter{Theoretical Foundations}\label{sec:theory}

\section{Principal Component Analysis}
\label{sec:theory:pca}
Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is a statistical technique used to reduce the dimensionality of a dataset while preserving most of the variance. This is achieved by identifying the principal axes, which are the directions in which the data varies the most. These directions corresond to the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix of the data, with the associated eigenvalues representing the magnitude of variance along these directions. PCA is particularly useful for dimensionality reduction, data compression, feature extraction and visualization of high-dimensional datasets. 

The origins of PCA can be traced back to \citet{pearson_lines_1901} in 1901 and \citet{hotelling_relations_1936} in 1936. It gained significant traction in meteorology through the work of \citet{lorenz_empirical_1956} in 1956, who introducted the technique under the term \textit{empirical orthogoanal function} (EOF) analysis\footnote{Although EOF analysis remains the preferred term in meteorology and climate science, we will use the more commonly recognized term, PCA.}. The method has since been extensively applied in meterology and climate science to identifiy dominant modes of variability in the atmosphere and oceans, as discussed in numerous textbooks \cite[e.g.][]{preisendorfer_principal_1988,jolliffe_principal_2002,storch_statistical_2003,hannachi_patterns_2021,wilks_statistical_2019,delsole_statistical_2022}. 

In this section, we derive PCA from the maximum variance formulation\footnote{\citet{hotelling_relations_1936} originally proposed this formulation. An alternative derivation, known as the minimum error formulation by \citet{pearson_lines_1901}, seeks a linear projection that minimizes the average projection error, defined as the mean squared distance between the original data points and their projections.}, as outlined by \citet[][pp.~561-563]{bishop_pattern_2006}. This approach involves projecting the data onto a lower-dimensiona linear space, known as the principal subspace, to maximize the variance of the projected data. We will then explore the limitations of PCA, particularly in the context of climate science, and discuss common strategies to address these limitations.

\subsection{Derivation from Maximum Variance Formulation}
Consider a data set represented as a complex-valued, two-dimensional $N\times D$ matrix $\bm{Z}$, where $N$ denotes the number of observations (or samples) and $D$ the number of variables (or features). We will refer to the $n$\textsuperscript{th} observation as the $D$-dimensional vector $\bm{z}_n$. For simplicity, we assume the data is centered, meaning the mean of each variable is zero, i.e. $\bar{\bm{z}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=1}^{N} \bm{z}_n = \bm{0}$. The goal is to project the data onto an $M$-dimensional space ($M<D$) while maximizing the variance of the projected data. 

To start, consider a one-dimensional projection ($M=1$). We define the direction of this space using a $D$-dimensional unit\footnote{This normalization is for convenience and not necessary, since we are only interested in the direction of $\bm{v}_1$, not its magnitude.} vector $\bm{v}_1$, such that $\bm{v}_1^H\bm{v}_1=1$, where $\bm{v}_1^H$ denotes the conjugate transpose of $\bm{v}_1$. The data points $\bm{z}_n$ are then projected onto this direction, $\bm{v}_1^H\bm{z}_n$, and the variance of the projected data is 

\begin{align}
    \frac{1}{N-1} \sum_{n=1}^{N-1} \ab( \bm{v}_1^H\bm{z}_n - \bm{v}_1^H\bar{\bm{z}} ) \ab( \bm{v}_1^H\bm{z}_n - \bm{v}_1^H\bar{\bm{z}} )^H = \bm{v}_1^H\bm{S}_{ZZ}\bm{v}_1,
\end{align}

where $\bm{S}_{ZZ}$ is the covariance matrix of $\bm{Z}$ given by

\begin{align}
    \bm{S}_{ZZ} = \frac{1}{N-1} \sum_{n=1}^{N} \bm{z}_n \bm{z}_n^H = \frac{1}{N-1} \bm{Z}^H \bm{Z}.
\end{align}

The goal is to find the vector $\bm{v}_1$ that maimizes this variance, formulated as:

\begin{align}\label{eq:theory:pca_maximization}
    \argmax_{\bm{v}_1} \, \bm{v}_1^H\bm{S}_{ZZ}\bm{v}_1 \quad \text{subject to} \quad \bm{v}_1^H\bm{v}_1 = 1.
\end{align}

To solve this optimization problem, we maximize the projected variance $\bm{v}_1^H\bm{S}_{ZZ}\bm{v}_1$ with respect to $\bm{v}_1$. To enforce the normalization constraint $\bm{v}_1^H\bm{v}_1=1$, a Lagrange multiplier $\lambda_1$ is introduced, leading to the unconstrained maximization of: 

\begin{align}
    \bm{v}_1^H\bm{S}_{ZZ}\bm{v}_1 + \lambda_1(1-\bm{v}_1^H\bm{v}_1).
\end{align}

By setting the derivative with respect to $\bm{v}_1$ to zero, we find a stationary point when: 

\begin{align}\label{eq:theory:pca_eigenproblem}
    \bm{S}_{ZZ}\bm{v}_1 = \lambda_1\bm{v}_1.
\end{align}

This indicates that $\bm{v}_1$ must be a eigenvector of $\bm{S}_{ZZ}$, with $\lambda_1$ being the corresponding real-valued\footnote{This follows from the property of the covariance matrix being hermitian.} eigenvalue. The variance $\bm{v}_1^T\bm{S}_{ZZ}\bm{v}_1$ reaches its maximum when $\bm{v}_1$ aligns with the eigenvector associated  with the largest eigenvalue\footnote{This can easily verified by left-multiplying Equation~\eqref{eq:theory:pca_eigenproblem} with $\bm{v}_1^H$ and making use of the normalization condition.} $\lambda_1$. The eigenvector is referred to as the first \textbf{vector of coefficients}\footnote{Naming conventions can vary significantly across different scientific domains. For example, in machine learning, the terms principal components and principal component scores refer to the eigenvectors (here $\bm{V}$) and their corresponding data projections (here $\bm{P}$)\cite[e.g.][]{bishop_pattern_2006}. In statistics, these are often called vectors of coefficients/loadings and principal components\cite[e.g.][]{jolliffe_principal_2002}, while in climate science and meteorology, they are known as empirical orthogonal functions and principal components/expansion coefficients~\cite[e.g.][]{storch_statistical_2003}. In this thesis, I aim to maintain consistency with the terminology commonly used in statistics.} (VC) of the data set.

To find additional VCs, we iteratively select new directions that maximize the projected variance while being orthogonal to the previously chosen directions. For an $M$-dimensional projection space, the optimal linear projection that maximimzes the variance of the projected data is defined by the $M$ eigenvectors $\bm{v}_1,\dots,\bm{v}_M$ of the covariance matrix $\bm{S}_{ZZ}$, corresponding to the $M$ largest eigenvalues $\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_M$. This result can be derived using proof by induction, leading to the concise expression:

\begin{align}\label{eq:theory:pca_eigenvector_decomposition}
    \bm{S}_{ZZ}  \bm{V} = \bm{V} \bm{\Lambda},
\end{align}

where $\bm{V}$ is unitary, i.e. $\bm{V}^T\bm{V}=\bm{I}_M$.

Thus, the eigenvectors of $\bm{S}_{ZZ}$ emerge as the new coordiante system "along" regularities in the data. For cases where these regularities are not aligned in unitary directions, see Section~\ref{sec:theory:rotated_pca}.

According to the spectral theorem for normal matrices~\cite[e.g.][p. 133]{horn_matrix_2012}, the hermitian covariance matrix $\bm{S}_{ZZ}$ can be decomposed into the sum of the outer products of the eigenvectors $\bm{v}_i$ and the corresponding real-valued eigenvalues $\lambda_i$:

\begin{align}
    \bm{S}_{ZZ} = \sum_{i=1}^M \lambda_i \bm{v}_i \bm{v}_i^H.
\end{align}

Each eigenvalue $\lambda_i$ represents the amount of variance captured by the corresponding component, typically expressed as a fraction $\gamma_i$ of the total variance:

\begin{align}
    \gamma_i = \frac{\lambda_i}{\sum_{i=1}^M\lambda_i}
\end{align}

Projecting the data $\bm{Z}$ onto the $M$-dimensional subspace thus provides a lower dimensional representation, called the \text{principal components} (PCs),

\begin{equation}
    \bm{P} = \bm{Z} \bm{V},
\end{equation}

which also enables the reconstruction of the original data from the projected  data:

\begin{align}\label{eq:theory:pca_reconstruction}
    \bm{Z} = \bm{P} \bm{V}^H.
\end{align}

However, calculating the eigenvector decomposition is not always computationally efficient. A more efficient method involves using Singular Value Domposition (SVD)~\cite[e.g.][p. 150]{horn_matrix_2012}, which decomposes the data matrix as

\begin{align}\label{eq:theory:pca_svd}
    \bm{Z} = \bm{U}' \bm{\Sigma} {\bm{V}'}^H,
\end{align}

where $\bm{U}'$ and $\bm{V}'$ are unitary matrices, ${\bm{U}'}^H \bm{U}' = {\bm{V}'}^H \bm{V}' = \bm{I}_M$, and $\bm{\Sigma}$ is a diagonal matrix containing the singular values $\sigma_i$ of $\bm{Z}$. The covariance matrix $\bm{S}_{ZZ}$ can then be expressed in terms of the SVD as

\begin{align}
    \bm{S} = \frac{1}{N-1} \bm{Z}^H \bm{Z} = \frac{1}{N-1} \bm{V}' \bm{\Sigma}^H \bm{U}'^H \bm{U}' \bm{\Sigma} \bm{V}'^H = \bm{V} \bm{\Lambda} {\bm{V}}^H,
\end{align}

where, by comparison with \eqref{eq:theory:pca_eigenvector_decomposition}, $\bm{V} = \bm{V}'$ and $\bm{\Lambda} = \frac{1}{N-1} \bm{\Sigma}^T \bm{\Sigma}$. This establishes a direct relationship between the eigenvalues of the covariance matrix and the singular values of the data matrix:

\begin{align}
    \lambda_i = \frac{ \sigma_i^2}{N-1}.
\end{align}

Comparing \eqref{eq:theory:pca_reconstruction} with \eqref{eq:theory:pca_svd} shows that the PC scores $\bm{P}$ can be normalized

\begin{align}
    \bm{U} &\equiv \bm{U}'= \bm{P} \bm{\Sigma}^{-1} = \frac{1}{\ab(N-1)^{1/2}} \bm{P} \bm{\Lambda}^{-1/2}, \nonumber \\
    \text{or alternatively} \quad \bm{P} &= \ab(N-1)^{1/2} \bm{U} \bm{\Lambda}^{1/2}.
\end{align}

Thus, $\bm{U}^H\bm{U} = \bm{I}_M$ ensures that the PC scores $\bm{U}$ are unitary and uncorrelated.

A common scaling method for the vector of coefficients $\bm{V}$ involves scaling by the square root of the corresponding eigenvalues, referred to as \textbf{loadings}:

\begin{align}
    \tilde{\bm{V}} = \bm{V} \bm{\Lambda}^{1/2}.
\end{align}

Although not strictly unitary, these loadings form a generalized unitary matrix, as shown by

\begin{align}\label{eq:theory:pca_loadings_non_orthogonal}
    \tilde{\bm{V}}^H \tilde{\bm{V}} = \bm{\Lambda}.
\end{align}
Using this scaling, the Equation~\ref{eq:theory:pca_reconstruction} can be expressed as

\begin{align}\label{eq:theory:pca_reconstruction_loadings}
    \bm{X} = \ab(N-1)^{1/2} \bm{U} \tilde{\bm{V}}^T.
\end{align}


\paragraph{Computational Considerations}

The computational cost of performing eigendencomposition on the covariance matrix scales with $\order*{D^3}$, making it impractical for modern datasets, such as those in climate science, where typical data sizes are on the order of $D=\order*{10^6}$ or even larger. For example, ERA5 \cite{c3s_era5_2018}, a widely used reference for state-of-the-art reanalysis climate data, distributes a single climate variable on a regular \ang{0.25} grid, resulting in $D=\num{1036800}$ grid cells.

In practice, more efficient algorithms, such as SVD or power methods~\cite{golub_matrix_2013}, have been employed traditionally employed. Recently,randomized low-rank approximation techniques~\cite{drineas_clustering_2004,halko_finding_2011} have further reduced computational complexity to $\order*{ND \log(M) + \ab(N+D)M^2}$. These advancements offer feasible alternatives for handling large, out-of-memory datasets (see Table~\ref{tab:pca_algorithm_complexity}).

\paragraph{Shortcomings} 

Despite its widespread use and versatility, PCA has several limitations and potential drawbacks that should be considered:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Linearity Assumption:} PCA assumes a linear structure in the data, which may not hold in complex systems such as climate data, where nonlinear relationships can be significant~\cite{scholkopf_nonlinear_1998}.
    \item \textbf{Interpretability of VCs:} The orthogonality of the vectors of coefficients is a mathematical construct that may not always correspond to the physical reality of the data, potentially leading to misinterpretations~\cite{richman_rotation_1986}.
    \item \textbf{Variance Focus:} Focusing on directions of maximum variance may not always capture the most relevant features for a specific application, potentially critical but less variable information~\cite{farrell_accurate_2001}.
    \item \textbf{Static Nature:} As a static method, PCA does not account for temporal dynamics or changes over time, which is particularly limiting in climate science, where time evolution is a key aspect.
    \item \textbf{Sensitivity to Outliers:} PCA can be sensitive to outliers, as extreme values can disproportionately influence the VCs, leading to misleading results.
    \item \textbf{Sensitivity to Scaling:} The results are sensitive to the scaling of the input data, meaning that variables with larger ranges can dominate the VCs unless the data is appropriately standardized.
    \item \textbf{Loss of Information:} While dimensionality reduction is often the goal, PCA inherently discards some information, which could lead to the loss of important details crucial for accurate analysis and interpretation in specific applications.
\end{enumerate}

Over the past few decades, numerous modifications and extensions have been proposed to address these limiations, especially in the context of climate science. For instance, to address the linearity assumption, various forms of kernel PCA \cite{webb_approach_1996,scholkopf_nonlinear_1998,bueso_nonlinear_2020} have been developed. For improved interpretability, rotated~\cite{richman_rotation_1986} and sparse~\cite{jolliffe_simplified_2002} PCA methods have been proposed. Robust PCA~\cite{candes_robust_2011} offers a more resilient approach to handling sensitivity to outlier. Furthermore, extended~\cite{weare_examples_1982,broomhead_extracting_1986} and complex~\cite{barnett_interaction_1983,horel_complex_1984} PCA methods have been introduced to account for temporal dynamics. In addition, eigenmethods like Optimal Persistence Analysis (OPA)~\cite{delsole_optimally_2001,delsole_low-frequency_2006} and Principal Oscillation Pattern (POP) analysis~\cite{hasselmann_pips_1988} provide alternative approaches independent to variance maximization to understanding dominant patterns in physical climate fields.

In the following sections, we will explore some of these shortcomings and proposed solutions in greater detail. Specifically, we will discuss how additional rotation of the PCA solution can enhance physical interpretability (Section~\ref{sec:theory:rotated_pca}) and how complexifying the data can provide insights into time evolution patterns (Section~\ref{sec:theory:hilbert_pca}).

\subsection{Sparse PCA via Rotation}\label{sec:theory:rotated_pca}
PCA is widely used for dimensionality reduction, as it maximizes the explained variance and provides orthogonality, making it optimal in a least-squares sense~\cite[][]{bishop_pattern_2006}. It effectively identifies principal axes that capture the covariance among variables, allowing for the examination of interrelationships between stations, grid points or variables. However, PCA is not ideal for identifiying the cause of variation or deriving physical modes of variability~\cite{richman_rotation_1986,legates_effect_1993,jolliffe_principal_2002}. 

One limitation is that real-world processes do not necessarily adhere to the orthogonality imposed by PCA's eigenvectors. Additionally, the dense nature of these VCs -- where all elements are non-zero -- can make interpretation challenging. In the context of climate and environmental science, where variables are often represented across spatial grids, these limitations can lead to recognizable but artificial patterns known as Buell patterns~\cite{buell_physical_1979,richman_rotation_1986,legates_effect_1991}. These patterns typically start with a positive component and evolve into increasingly complex structures, such as bipole or tripole patterns (Figure~\ref{fig:theory:pca_buell_patterns}). Such issues highlight the disconnect between standard PCA and underlying physical processes~\cite{richman_rotation_1986}, prompting the development of alternative methods that produce sparse VCs, characterized by many zero or near-zero elements. This sparsity -- which necessarily implies the loss of orthogonality for at least one of the VCs and PC scores -- can enhance interpretability by localizing the patterns and reducing the density of the VCs.

In climate science, the most common approach to achieving sparse PCA is through rotation techniques~\cite{horel_rotated_1981,richman_obliquely_1981,richman_rotation_1986}, a concept borrowed from factor analysis \cite[cf.][pp. 269]{jolliffe_principal_2002}. In this "factor analysis viewpoint" of PCA \cite[][pp. 150-161]{jolliffe_principal_2002}, a linear transformation is applied to a subset of the VCs to derive a new set of rotated VCs that are "simpler" in a heuristically defined manner, ideally aligning better with the underlying physical processes. 

As noted in Equation~\eqref{eq:theory:pca_reconstruction_loadings}, once the orthogonality constraint is relaxed, PCA solutions exhibit inherent indeterminacy, allowing any non-singular transformation $\bm{R}$ to satisfy the PCA formulation \cite[e.g.][p. 305]{richman_rotation_1986}:

\begin{align}
    \bm{Z} = \ab(N-1)^{1/2} \bm{U} \tilde{\bm{V}}^H = \ab(N-1)^{1/2} \bm{U} \bm{R} \bm{R}^{-1} \tilde{\bm{V}}^H.
\end{align}

Rotated PCA exploits this indeterminacy by choosing $\bm{R}$ to optimize a simplicity criterion $f$, resulting in rotated loadings\footnote{In principle, it is possible to rotate the unitary matrix of coefficients $\bm{V}$ instead of the loadings matrix $\tilde{\bm{V}}$~\cite{mestasnunez_orthogonality_2000,jolliffe_principal_2002}. However, this approach is seldom used in practice. The primary reason is that rotating the unitary coefficient matrix preserves the orthogonality of the VCs but results in the loss of orthogonality among the PCs, which does not address issues like the Buell patterns. Moreover, this method does not consider the relative importance of the spatial patterns, as indicated by the eigenvalues, making the results of the rotation process highly sensitive to the selection of the number of components to rotate.} $\tilde{\bm{V}}_R = \tilde{\bm{V}} \bm{R}$ that maximize $f$:

\begin{align}
    \argmax_{\bm{R}} \, f(\tilde{\bm{V}}_R).
\end{align}


There are two main categories of rotation: \textit{orthogonal}\footnote{In the broader context of complex data, this would be referred to as a unitary rotation. However, since factor analysis has primarily been applied to real-valued data, the terms orthogonal and oblique rotations have become the standard. To avoid confusion, I use these historical terms while acknowledging that they also encompass the more general case of complex data.} and \textit{oblique}. In orthogonal rotation, $\bm{R}$ is a unitary matrix, preserving the uncorrelated nature of the PCs since $\bm{U}_R^H\bm{U}_R = \bm{R}^H\bm{U}^H\bm{U}\bm{R} = \bm{1}_M$. In oblique rotation, $\bm{R}$ is a gneral non-singular matrix, resulting in correlated PCs, as $\bm{U}_R^H\bm{U}_R = \bm{R}^H\bm{R}$.
Regardless of the method, the rotated loadings are not orthogonal,

\begin{align}
    \tilde{\bm{V}}_R^H \tilde{\bm{V}}_R = \bm{R}^H \tilde{\bm{V}}^H  \tilde{\bm{V}} \bm{R} \overset{\eqref{eq:theory:pca_loadings_non_orthogonal}}{=} \bm{R}^H \bm{\Lambda} \bm{R} \neq \bm{I}_M,
\end{align}

which is crucial for achieving a more interpretable solution. Various simplicity criteria $f$ have been proposed~\cite[see e.g.][]{richman_rotation_1986}, with most focusing on making the rotated loadings more localized and physically interpretable. In practice, the specific choice of simplicity criterion often has little impact on the results~\cite{richman_rotation_1986}.

\paragraph{Varimax Orthogonal Rotation} The most widely used orthogonal rotation technique is the varimax criterion~\cite{kaiser_varimax_1958}, which aims to simplify the VCs by maximizing the variance of the squared loadings,

\begin{align}
    f(\tilde{\bm{V}}_R) = \sum_{m=1}^{M} \ab(p \sum_{j=1}^{P} b_{jm}^2 - \ab(\sum_{j=1}^{P} b_{jm})^2 )
\end{align}

where $b_{jm}$ are the squared norm of the rotated loadings $|\tilde{v}_{R, jm}|^2 / h_j^2$ normalized by the \textit{communalities} $h_j^2 = \sum_{m=1}^{M}|\tilde{v}_{R, jm}|^2$ of variable $j$. The varimax rotation pushes the elements of $\tilde{\bm{V}}_R$ towards either 0 or 1, creating a sparse solution with primarily large or small values, which is particularly useful for identifying localized physical processes, such as atmospheric or oceanic teleconnections.

\paragraph{Promax Oblique Rotation} The promax criterion~\cite{hendrickson_promax_1964}, a generalization of the varimax criterion, is one of the most common oblique rotation techniques. It is based on the premise that an orthogonal simple structure solution, such as varimax, is often close to an optimal oblique solution. The promax method constructs a hypothetical oblique target matrix $T$ by performing an oblique Procrustes transformation~\cite{hurley_procrustes_1962} on the varimax-rotated loadings matrix,

\begin{align}\label{eq:theory:procrustes}
    T = \tilde{\bm{V}}_R \bm{R} + \bm{E},
\end{align}

where $\bm{R}$ is the transformation matrix and $\bm{E}$ is a matrix of residuals. The normal equation for solving the complex Procrustes problem is given by:

\begin{align}
    \bm{R} = (\tilde{\bm{V}}_R^H \tilde{\bm{V}}_R)^{-1} \bm{T}^H \tilde{\bm{V}}_R.
\end{align}

To define the target matrix $T$, the promax criterion increases the discrimination between high and low loadings by raising the loadings to a power $q$ while retaining the original sign,

\begin{align}
    t_{jm} = \frac{\ab|a_{jm}|^{q+1}}{a_{jm}},
\end{align}

where $q$ controls the degree of obliqueness and $a_{jm}$ are the elements of the max-normalized, varimax-rotated loadings matrix $\bm{A}$, defined as 

\begin{align}
    \bm{A} &= \tilde{\bm{A}} \bm{K}^{-1}, \\
    \tilde{\bm{A}} &= \bm{H}^{-1}\tilde{\bm{V}}_R,
\end{align}

with $\bm{K}$ being a diagonal matrix containing the maximum absolute values of each column of $\tilde{\bm{A}}$, $a_{m} = \max_{m} \ab|A_{pm}|$, and $\bm{H}$ a diagonal matrix with the square root of the communalities $h_j$ for the $j$\textsuperscript{th} variable.

\paragraph{Application and Criticism} Both orthogonal and oblique rotation techniques, particularly varimax and promax, have been extensively used since the 1980s to identify stationary modes of variability in climate data~\cite[e.g.][]{horel_rotated_1981,barnston_classification_1987,lim_east_2015,gao_impacts_2017,mezzina_dynamics_2020,huth_how_2021,lee_quantifying_2019}. Notable climate oscillations, such as the North Atlantic Oscillation (NAO),
have been defined based on varimax-rotated patterns of loadings~\cite{barnston_classification_1987}. These techniques also help identify "ghost patterns", artificial patterns identified by PCA that are not physically meaningful~\cite{lian_evaluation_2012,huth_how_2021}.

Rotated modes offer several advantages, such as greater robustness against 
temporal and spatial subsampling~\cite{cheng_robustness_1995} compared to unrotated modes. Thy also effectively mitigate the statistical artifacts assocaited with Buell patterns, resulting in more realistic representations of the underlying autocorrelation structure and thus increased interpretability~\cite{richman_climatic_1985,richman_rotation_1986,lian_evaluation_2012}. Moreover, rotation techniques have been successfully interated into other PCa extensions dealing with the non-linear and non-stationary nature of climate oscillations~\cite{horel_complex_1984,bueso_nonlinear_2020,guilloteau_rotated_2020}.

However, rotation techniques are not without criticism, mainly due to the ad-hoc nature of the various simplicity criteria~\cite[cf.][pp. 270-274]{jolliffe_principal_2002}. This has led to the exploration of alternative approaches to enforce sparsity. Notably, \citet{jolliffe_simplified_2002} introduced the Simplified Component Technique - LASSO (SCoTLASS), a sparse PCA variant based on the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO), originally from regularized regression \cite{tibshirani_regression_1996}. This method imopses a constraint on the $\ell_1$ norm of the vector of loadings, resulting in some loading coefficients being exactly zero.

Despite its advantages, SCoTLASS has drawbacks, particularly when the number of variables $D$ is much larger than the number of observations $N$. In such cases, SCoTLASS can only select at most $N$ non-zero elements in the loading vectors, which is not ideal in climate science where often $D>>N$. Additionally, since SCoTLASS solutions cannot be obtained via SVD and require a numerical approach using projected gradient methods~\cite{trendafilov_projected_2006}, the computational demand is typically higher 
than for (rotated) PCA. While this method has inspired many other sparse PCA techniques based on penalties or constraints (usually $\ell_0$ or $\ell_1$)~\cite[see e.g.][]{zou_sparse_2006,zou_selective_2018,erichson_sparse_2020}, these formulations are often complex and require algorithmic innovations to avoid issues like local optima and ensure convergence. They may also face challenges with memory limitations, slow computation speeds and limited effectiveness in modelling the data~\cite{guerra-urzola_guide_2021,camacho_all_2020}.

It is likely for these reasons, that rotated PCA remains one of the most commonly used techniques for achieving sparse PCA solutions in climate science. It is computationlly efficient, straightforward to implement and provides a more interpretable understanding of the data compared to standard PCA.

\subsection{Hilbert PCA}\label{sec:theory:hilbert_pca}
While PCA efficiently identifies the principal axes along which the covariance among all variables is distributed, and rotated PCA provides a more sprase solution related to underlying physical processes, both methods are inherently staatic. They do not for temporal dynamics or changes over time, as they only consider the current state of the physical field. This limitation is significant in climate science, where time evolution is crucial. For instance, standard PCA does not differentiate between the order of data points over time, resulting in the same eigenvectors and eigenvalues regardless of temporal permutations. 

This static nature poses a challenge for capturing osciallatory patterns, such as QBO, MJO, ENSO and BSISO, which involve spatial patterns propagating over time. These phenomena cannot be adequately captured by standard PCA.

\subsubsection{Hilbert Transform as a Momentum Operator}

To address this limitation, one approach is to model both the current state of the climate and its tendency or 'momentum', analogous to the Hamiltonian principle in mechanics, where a system's future is decribed by first-order differential equations for location (state) and momentum (tendency)~\cite[cf.][p. 353]{storch_statistical_2003}. In classical mechanics, propagating features are represented using canonical coordinates: each state variable is augmented by a momentum variable, allowing oscillations to be depicted as rotations in a two-dimensional phase space. From a data perspective, this augmentation can be achieved via the Hilbert transform ($\mathcal{H}$). For a real time series $x(t)$, the Hilbert transform $x'(t)$ is defined as


\begin{align}\label{eq:theory:hilbert_transform_time}
x'(t) \equiv \HT{x}(t) = \frac{1}{\pi} \pv \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{x(\tau)}{\tau - t} d\tau,
\end{align}

where $\pv$ denotes the Cauchy principal value ensuring convergence despite the singularity at $\tau=t$. The Hilbert transform emphazises the local properties of $x(t)$ by convolution with $\frac{1}{\pi t}$. In the frequency domain, it is related to the Fourier transform $\mathcal{F}$ by

\begin{align}\label{eq:theory:hilbert_transform_frequency}
    \FT{y}(\omega) = -i \sgn (\omega) \FT{x}(\omega)
\end{align}

where the imaginary unit $i$ indicates a phase shift of $\pi/2$ for the frequency components $\omega$ of $x$, placing $x'$ in quadrature to $x$. This provides an intuitive interpretation of the Hilbert transform, as it acts as a linear filter that alters the phase of the signal's components by 90 degrees without changing their amplitude.

By using the Hilbert transform, a real-valued time series $x(t)$ can be complexified into $z(t) = x(t) + i \HT{x}(t)$, known as the \textit{analytic signal}, which allows for the computation of the instantenous amplitude and phase of $x(t)$:

\begin{align}
    A(t) = |z(t)| = \sqrt{x(t)^2 + \HT{x}(t)^2}, \quad \phi(t) = \arg(z(t)) = \arctan \left( \frac{\HT{x}(t)}{x(t)} \right).
\end{align}

Similar results can be obtained for the discrete case~\cite[][pp. 942-956]{oppenheim_discrete-time_2010}, and this concept can be extended to multivariate data~\cite[e.g.][pp. 105-106]{hannachi_patterns_2021}, 
defining the analytic signal of a data matrix $X$ as

\begin{align}
    \bm{Z} = \bm{X} + i \HT{\bm{X}},
\end{align}

where $\HT{\bm{X}}$ is the feature-wise Hilbert transform of $\bm{X}$. 


\citet{rasmusson_biennial_1981} introduced the concept of Hilbert PCA using this data augmentation technique \footnote{Although \citet{rasmusson_biennial_1981} originally termed it \textit{Hilbert Singular Decomposition}, the terms \textit{Hilbert EOF/PCA} and \textit{Complex EOF} anaylsis are more commonly used in the literature \cite{jolliffe_principal_2002,storch_statistical_2003,hannachi_patterns_2021,burger_intraseasonal_2021}.}. This method, refined by \citet{barnett_interaction_1983} and \citet{horel_complex_1984}, involves applying PCA to the augmented data matrix $\bm{Z}$, By incorporating phase information, Hilbert PCA can capture the direction and speed of wave-like patterns, unlike traditional PCA, which only identifies standing oscillations. The resulting eigenvectors and principal component scores are complex, with the real and imaginary components linked via the Hilbert transform. The variance capture by each component is represented by the real eigenvalues of the complex covariance matrix of $\bm{Z}$ (see Section~\ref{sec:theory:pca}).

The main advantage of Hilbert PCA is the ability to identify and reconstruct propagating waves, which is especially valuable in studying climate phenomena characterized by such patterns, including MJO, ENSO, QBO and BSISO. For instance, \citet{cai_forcing_2001} utilized Hilbert PCA to analyze SST in the southern hemisphere, linking the findings to ENSO, while \citet{burger_intraseasonal_2021} employed it to develop an index for BSISO. However, to reliably identify oscillatory patterns, sufficiently long time series are required to resolve at least one full oscillation~\cite{merrifield_detecting_1990}. Additionally, interpreting of the Hilbert transform as representing the instantenous frequency is valid holds for narrow-band signals; the phase of the Hilbert transform is not well-defined for broadband signals~\cite{gabor_theory_1946,boashash_estimating_1992}. Moreover, real-time monitoring is challenging with Hilbert PCA because new real-valued data cannot be directly projected onto the existing patterns without knowing or estimating the exact momentum (phase) of the new data, which requires future information (Equation~\eqref{eq:theory:hilbert_transform_time}). To mitigate this, an approximative real-time (causal) Hilbert transform can be employed~\cite{burger_complex_1993,burger_intraseasonal_2021}, offering a practical remedy to the issue of real-time applicability. Finally, Hilbert PCA shares PCA's limitations regarding physical interpretability due to the orthogonality of the VCs. To address this, rotated Hilbert PCA has been proposed to enhance interpretability~\cite{horel_rotated_1981,bloomfield_orthogonal_1994}.

\section{Maximum Covariance Analysis (MCA)}

Sometimes we're not so much interested in the primary modes of variations within a dataset, but rather the modes of shared variations between two different data sets. Consider the MJO, which is the largest element of the intraseasonal variability in the tropical atmosphere, characterized by an eastward migrating cell of overturning wind circulation within the troposphere that lead to large regions of enhanced and suppressed precipitation. Rather than interested in the dominant variability in any of the individual fields, we may want to find the shared dyanmics between these variables ignoring dominant variability that is associated only to one of the variables such as diurnal cycles linked to surface processes but which are absent in higher levels of the atmosphere.

Maximum Covariance Analysis (MCA) is a statistical method that has exactly this goal: to identify patterns of maximum covariance between two (or more) different datasets \cite[][p. 321]{storch_statistical_2003}. It is a data-driven approach similar to PCA, in the sense that we can formulate it as optimization problem where we want to find pairs of coupled (complex) patterns $\bm{v}_{i,1}$ for two (complex-valued) datasets, $\bm{Z}_i, i=\bab{x, y}$, such that the projection of the data onto these patterns maximizes the covariance between the two datasets,

\begin{align}\label{eq:theory:mca_maximization}
    \argmax_{\bm{v}_{x,i}} \, = \ab(\bm{v}_{x,1} \bm{C}_{xy} \bm{v}_{y,1})^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad \Vab{\bm{v}_{x,i}}_2 = 1,
\end{align}

where the cross-covariance matrix between the two datasets is given by

\begin{align}\label{eq:theory:mca_cross_covariance}
    \bm{C}_{xy} = \frac{1}{N-1} \bm{Z}_x^H \bm{Z}_y.
\end{align}

The solution to this problem gives rise to a generalized eigenvalue equations similar to that of PCA, which can also be solved by using the SVD of the cross-covariance matrix

\begin{align}\label{eq:theory:mca_svd}
    \bm{C}_{xy} = \bm{V}_x \bm{\Sigma} \bm{V}_y^H
\end{align}

% Proof that optimality of leading SVD components: https://atmos.washington.edu/~breth/classes/AS552/lect/lect22.pdf

where the $m$\textsuperscript{th} column of the left and right singular vectors, $V_x$ and $V_y$, contains the patterns $\bm{v}_{x,m}$ and $\bm{v}_{y,m}$, and $\bm{\Sigma}$ a diagonal matrix containing the singular values $\sigma_i$ in decending order. Then it can be easily verified that the first left and right singular vectors corresponding to the first column maximize Equation~\eqref{eq:theory:mca_maximization}, resulting in a corresponding maximum covariance given by the largest singular value $\sigma_1$.

Since the patterns are unitary, we can expand the datasets according to

\begin{align}
    \bm{Z}_i =& \bm{P}_i \bm{V}_i^H,
\end{align}

with the expansion coefficient $\bm{P}_i$. The expansion coefficients $\bm{P}_1$ and $\bm{P}_2$ are uncorrelated with each other, since

\begin{align}
    \bm{P}_1^H \bm{P}_2 =& \bm{V}_1^H \bm{Z}_1^H \bm{Z}_2 \bm{V}_2 \overset{\eqref{eq:theory:mca_cross_covariance}}{=} (N-1) \bm{V}_1^H \bm{C}_{xy} \bm{V}_2 \overset{\eqref{eq:theory:mca_svd}}{=} (N-1)\bm{\Sigma}.
\end{align}

In contrast, the expansion coefficients $\bm{P}_1$ and $\bm{P}_2$ are, in general, correlated with themselves, i.e. $\bm{P}_i^H\bm{P}_i \neq c \bm{I}_m$ for any arbitrary constant $c$.

The total covariance is given by squared Frobenius norm of the cross-covariance matrix, 

\begin{align}
    \Vab{\bm{C}_{xy}}_F^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{M} \sigma_i^2, 
\end{align}    

which shows that the $i$\textsuperscript{th} mode captures the squared covariance $\sigma_i^2$, thus its relative importance can be expressed as the squared covariance fraction $f_{xy, i}$:

\begin{align}
    f_{xy, i} = \frac{\sigma_i^2}{\sum_{i=1}^{M} \sigma_i^2}.
\end{align}

This provides a measure of the relative importance of the $i$\textsuperscript{th} mode in capturing the shared variability between the two datasets. However, to quantify the importance of the patterns themselves, we need to consider the variance within the individual datasets explained by each mode in relation to the total variance of the individual datasets, which can be expressed as the squared Frobenius norm of the individual datasets,

\begin{align}
    f_{xx, i} &= \frac{\bm{v}_{x,i}^H\bm{C}_{xx}\bm{v}_{x,i}}{\Vab{\bm{Z}_x}_F^2} = \frac{1}{N-1} \frac{\bm{p}_{x,i}^H\bm{p}_{x,i}}{\Vab{\bm{Z}_x}_F^2},\\
    f_{yy, i} &= \frac{\bm{v}_{y,i}^H\bm{C}_{yy}\bm{v}_{y,i}}{\Vab{\bm{Z}_y}_F^2} = \frac{1}{N-1} \frac{\bm{p}_{y,i}^H\bm{p}_{y,i}}{\Vab{\bm{Z}_y}_F^2}.
\end{align}

The method was popularized in the field of meterology\footnote{According to \citet[][p. 225]{jolliffe_principal_2002}, it was first used in psychology starting from the 1950s where it is known as inter-battery factor analysis. As a variant of Partial Least Squares (PLS), it was later used in biomedical sciences by \citet{sampson_neurobehavioral_1989} and termed PLS-SVD by \citet{wegelin_survey_2000}- In meteorology, \citet{bretherton_intercomparison_1992} coined the confusing term SVD analysis, which is still in use today \cite[e.g.][]{mori_reconciled_2019}. Rarely, the method is also called Canonical Covariance Analysis \cite[e.g.][]{cherry_singular_1996,hannachi_patterns_2021}, akin to Canonical Correlation Analysis.} by \citet{bretherton_intercomparison_1992} and \citet{wallace_singular_1992} since when it has been extensively applied \cite[e.g.][]{wallace_singular_1992,tsai_maximum_2009,li_north_2016,barreto_maximum_2017,mori_reconciled_2019,rathore_improving_2021}



Why Is It Important?
Understanding Coupled Variability: MCA is crucial for understanding the coupled variability between different climatic variables. For instance, it can help identify how changes in sea surface temperatures may be related to atmospheric pressure patterns, which is vital for understanding phenomena like El Niño-Southern Oscillation (ENSO).

Teleconnections Analysis: It is used to study teleconnections, which are climate anomalies related to each other at large distances (thousands of kilometers apart). By identifying the dominant modes of variability, MCA helps in understanding how different regions of the climate system interact.

Climate Prediction: The patterns identified by MCA can be used for predictive modeling. By understanding the leading modes of variability, scientists can make better predictions about future climate conditions.

Data Reduction and Interpretation: MCA helps in reducing the dimensionality of large climate datasets by focusing on the most significant modes of variability, making it easier to interpret complex interactions.

Whereas PCA works with a single set of random variables, MCA considers two (or more) variables and tries to find a corresponding pair of linear subspaces that have high cross-covariance, so that each component within one of the subspaces exhibits high covariance with a single component from the other subspace. Its solution can be expressed in terms of a generalized eigenvector problem.

The origins of Maximum Covariance Analysis (MCA) date back to Tucker (1958), where it is known as inter-battery factor analysis; The method was popularised in atmospheric science by Bretherton et al. (1992) and Wallace et al. (1992) under the name singular value decomposition (SVD) analysis. the name maximum covariance analysis is used by von Storch and Zwiers (1999, Section 14.1.7) 


MCA reduces to PCA when both sets are the same, but there's an even deeper link to PCA: maximum covariance analysis can be viewed as two PCAs, followed by rotation to match up the results of the two analyses as closely as possible (Cherry, 1997).


difference to CCA: 
Maximum covariance analysis diﬀers from CCA in two ways: covariance rather than correlation is maximized, and vectors of loadings are orthogonal instead of derived variates being uncorrelated. The rationale behind maximum covariance
analysis is that it may be important to explain a large proportion of
the variance in one set of variables using the other set, and a pair of
variates from CCA with large correlation need not necessarily have large
variance

Wilks:
There are two main distinctions between CCA and MCA. The first is that CCA maximizes correlation, whereas MCA maximizes covariance. The leading CCA modes may capture relatively little of the corresponding variances (and thus yield small covariances even if the canonical correlations are high). On the other hand, MCA will find linear combinations with large covariances, which may result
more from large variances than a large correlation. The second difference is that the vectors am and bm in maximum covariance analysis are orthogonal, and the projections vm and wm of the data onto them are in general correlated, whereas the canonical variates in CCA are uncorrelated but the corresponding
canonical vectors are not generally orthogonal. Bretherton et al. (1992), Cherry (1996), Tippett et al. (2008), and Van den Dool (2007) compare the two methods in greater detail.
