\part{Theoretical Foundations}\label{part:theory}

\chapter{Data-Driven Dimensionality Reduction}\label{chapter:dimred}

With the ever-growing volume of environmental data—particularly in climate science, meteorology, and oceanography—there is an increasing need for algorithms that can efficiently uncover relevant and interpretable patterns. These data-driven methods help researchers navigate massive datasets, such as ECMWF's ERA5 \cite{hersbach_era5_2020}, which already exceeds 9 PB of information, and the forthcoming ERA6 (planned for release in 2025) that is projected to be three to four times larger \cite{hans_hersbach_estimating_2025}. Although these comprehensive reanalysis products provide an unprecedented record of global climate processes at high temporal and spatial resolution, their sheer size and complexity pose significant challenges in extracting actionable insights.

A common strategy for dealing with large-scale datasets is to reduce their dimensionality so that only a small number of important factors or "latent modes" remain. This rests on the notion that climate fields often exhibit substantial redundancy: the vast number of spatio-temporal measurements are largely driven by a few dominant physical mechanisms (e.g., large-scale atmospheric or oceanic circulation patterns). In statistics, this principle is sometimes called \emph{factor sparsity}, suggesting that a small number of factors typically capture the bulk of the observed variability.

Against this backdrop, this chapter reviews two widely used techniques for dimensionality reduction in climate data: \emph{Principal Component Analysis} (PCA) and \emph{Maximum Covariance Analysis} (MCA). Although well-established, both methods remain foundational for modern, large-scale geophysical research \cite[e.g.][]{hannachi_patterns_2021,mori_reconciled_2019,di_capua_dominant_2020,rathore_improving_2021}. PCA identifies key modes of variability from a single dataset, whereas MCA generalizes this idea to two datasets, isolating cross-covarying patterns that link different climatic variables. We also discuss how these methods can be extended to capture propagating features using Hilbert transforms and to enhance interpretability via rotational methods that produce more localized or sparse patterns.

The main novel aspects developed here are: 
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em] 
    \item \emph{Rotated Hilbert MCA.} We extend the MCA framework with Hilbert (complex) transformations and subsequent rotations, similar to Hilbert PCA, enabling the detection of propagating, phase-shifted patterns that would otherwise remain hidden.
    \item \emph{Spectral Leakage Mitigation.} Through simulation studies, we demonstrate how spectral leakage can compromise Hilbert PCA and propose a practical time-series extension approach to mitigate this issue.
    \item \emph{Rotated MCA and Covariance Preservation.} We show theoretically that applying standard rotation methods in MCA does \emph{not} preserve the total \emph{squared} covariance—a subtle but important distinction for interpreting rotated MCA modes.
    \item \emph{Mode Selection Criterion.} In the framework of rotated MCA, we introduce a stability-based criterion for selecting the optimal number of rotated modes, improving the robustness and reliability of the extracted patterns.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of this chapter is structured as follows. In Section~\ref{sec:theory:pca}, we derive PCA from a maximum-variance perspective (Section~\ref{sec:theory:pca_derivation}) and describe its common extensions, including rotated PCA (Section~\ref{sec:theory:rotated_pca}) and Hilbert PCA (Section~\ref{sec:theory:hilbert_pca}). Section~\ref{sec:theory:mca} then introduces MCA, before focusing on rotated Hilbert MCA (Section~\ref{sec:theory:mca_rotated_hilbert}) and Continuum Power Canonical Correlation Analysis (CPCCA)---a more flexible framework for detecting either covariance- or correlation-focused modes (Section~\ref{sec:theory:cpcca}). In subsequent chapters, these methods are applied to real-world cases using the open-source \software{xeofs} library (Chapter~\ref{chapter:xeofs}) and showcased in global teleconnection studies (Chapter~\ref{chapter:tele}).


\section{Principal Component Analysis}
\label{sec:theory:pca}
Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is a statistical technique used to reduce the dimensionality of a dataset while preserving most of the variance. This is achieved by identifying the principal axes, which are the directions in which the data varies the most. These directions correspond to the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix of the data, with the associated eigenvalues representing the magnitude of variance along these directions. PCA is particularly useful for dimensionality reduction, data compression, feature extraction and visualization of high-dimensional datasets. 

The origins of PCA can be traced back to \citet{pearson_lines_1901} in 1901 and \citet{hotelling_relations_1936} in 1936. It gained significant traction in meteorology through the work of \citet{lorenz_empirical_1956} in 1956, who introduced the technique under the term \textit{empirical orthogonal function} (EOF) analysis\footnote{Although EOF analysis remains the preferred term in meteorology and climate science, we will use the more commonly recognized term, PCA.}. The method has since been extensively applied in meteorology and climate science to identify dominant modes of variability in the atmosphere and oceans, as discussed in numerous textbooks \cite[e.g.][]{preisendorfer_principal_1988,jolliffe_principal_2002,storch_statistical_1999,hannachi_patterns_2021,wilks_statistical_2019,delsole_statistical_2022}. 

In this section, we derive PCA from the maximum variance formulation\footnote{\citet{hotelling_relations_1936} originally proposed this formulation. An alternative derivation, known as the minimum error formulation by \citet{pearson_lines_1901}, seeks a linear projection that minimizes the average projection error, defined as the mean squared distance between the original data points and their projections. This alternative approach shows that PCA is an optimal solution for approximating data in the squared error sense.}, as outlined by \citet[][pp.~561-563]{bishop_pattern_2006}. This approach involves projecting the data onto a lower-dimensional linear space, known as the principal subspace, to maximize the variance of the projected data. We will then explore the limitations of PCA, particularly in the context of climate science, and discuss common strategies to address these limitations, such as rotated PCA and Hilbert PCA.


\subsection{Derivation from Maximum Variance Formulation}\label{sec:theory:pca_derivation}
Consider a data set represented as a complex-valued, two-dimensional $N\times D$ matrix $\bm{Z}$, where $N$ denotes the number of observations (or samples) and $D$ the number of variables (or features). We will refer to the $n$\textsuperscript{th} observation as the $D$-dimensional vector $\bm{z}_n$. For simplicity, we assume the data is centered, meaning the mean of each variable is zero, i.e. $\bar{\bm{z}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=1}^{N} \bm{z}_n = \bm{0}$. The goal is to project the data onto an $M$-dimensional space ($M<D$) of $M$ latent variables while maximizing the variance of the projected data.

To start, consider a one-dimensional projection ($M=1$). We define the direction of this space using a $D$-dimensional unit\footnote{This particular normalization is for convenience, and any other would do just fine, since we are only interested in the direction of $\bm{v}_1$, not its magnitude.} vector $\bm{v}_1$, such that $\bm{v}_1^H\bm{v}_1=1$, where $\bm{v}_1^H$ denotes the conjugate transpose of $\bm{v}_1$. The data points $\bm{z}_n$ are then projected onto this direction, $\bm{v}_1^H\bm{z}_n$, and the variance of the projected data is 

\begin{align}
    \frac{1}{N-1} \sum_{n=1}^{N-1} \ab( \bm{v}_1^H\bm{z}_n - \bm{v}_1^H\bar{\bm{z}} )^2 = \bm{v}_1^H\bm{S}_{ZZ}\bm{v}_1,
\end{align}

where $\bm{S}_{ZZ}$ is the covariance matrix of $\bm{Z}$ given by

\begin{align}
    \bm{S}_{ZZ} = \frac{1}{N-1} \sum_{n=1}^{N} \bm{z}_n \bm{z}_n^H = \frac{1}{N-1} \bm{Z}^H \bm{Z}.
\end{align}

The goal is to find the vector $\bm{v}_1$ that maximizes this variance, formulated as:

\begin{align}\label{eq:theory:pca_maximization}
    \argmax_{\bm{v}_1} \, \bm{v}_1^H\bm{S}_{ZZ}\bm{v}_1 \quad \text{subject to} \quad \bm{v}_1^H\bm{v}_1 = 1.
\end{align}

To solve this optimization problem, we maximize the projected variance $\bm{v}_1^H\bm{S}_{ZZ}\bm{v}_1$ with respect to $\bm{v}_1$. To enforce the normalization constraint $\bm{v}_1^H\bm{v}_1=1$, a Lagrange multiplier $\lambda_1$ is introduced, leading to the unconstrained maximization of: 

\begin{align}
    \bm{v}_1^H\bm{S}_{ZZ}\bm{v}_1 + \lambda_1(1-\bm{v}_1^H\bm{v}_1).
\end{align}

By setting the derivative with respect to $\bm{v}_1$ to zero, we find a stationary point when: 

\begin{align}\label{eq:theory:pca_eigenproblem}
    \bm{S}_{ZZ}\bm{v}_1 = \lambda_1\bm{v}_1.
\end{align}

This indicates that $\bm{v}_1$ must be an eigenvector of $\bm{S}_{ZZ}$, with $\lambda_1$ being the corresponding real-valued\footnote{This follows from the property of the covariance matrix being hermitian.} eigenvalue. The variance $\bm{v}_1^T\bm{S}_{ZZ}\bm{v}_1$ reaches its maximum when $\bm{v}_1$ aligns with the eigenvector associated  with the largest eigenvalue\footnote{This can easily be verified by left-multiplying Equation~\eqref{eq:theory:pca_eigenproblem} with $\bm{v}_1^H$ and making use of the normalization condition.} $\lambda_1$. The eigenvector is referred to as the first \textbf{vector of coefficients}\footnote{Naming conventions can vary significantly across different scientific domains. For example, in machine learning, the terms principal components and principal component scores refer to the eigenvectors (here $\bm{V}$) and their corresponding data projections (here $\bm{P}$)\cite[e.g.][]{bishop_pattern_2006}. In statistics, these are often called vectors of coefficients/loadings and principal components\cite[e.g.][]{jolliffe_principal_2002}, while in climate science and meteorology, they are known as empirical orthogonal functions and principal components/expansion coefficients~\cite[e.g.][]{storch_statistical_1999}. In this thesis, I aim to maintain consistency with the terminology commonly used in statistics.} (VC) of the data set.

To find additional VCs, we iteratively select new directions that maximize the projected variance while being orthogonal to the previously chosen directions. For an $M$-dimensional projection space, the optimal linear projection that maximizes the variance of the projected data is defined by the $M$ eigenvectors $\bm{v}_1,\dots,\bm{v}_M$ of the covariance matrix $\bm{S}_{ZZ}$, corresponding to the $M$ largest eigenvalues $\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_M$. This result can be derived using proof by induction, leading to the concise expression:

\begin{align}\label{eq:theory:pca_eigenvector_decomposition}
    \bm{S}_{ZZ}  \bm{V} = \bm{V} \bm{\Lambda},
\end{align}

where $\bm{V}$ is unitary, i.e. $\bm{V}^H\bm{V}=\bm{I}_M$, and $\Lambda=\diag(\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_M)$ is the diagonal matrix of eigenvalues.

Thus, the eigenvectors of $\bm{S}_{ZZ}$ define a new unitary coordinate system that aligns with the primary patterns of variability in the data. In other words, PCA identifies the directions (principal axes) along which the data exhibits the most significant variations. However, these principal components are constrained to be orthogonal to each other. In situations where the underlying patterns in the data are not naturally aligned with these orthogonal directions, a rotation technique such as varimax (discussed in Section~\ref{sec:theory:rotated_pca}) can be applied to achieve a more interpretable solution.

According to the spectral theorem for normal matrices~\cite[e.g.][p. 133]{horn_matrix_2012}, the hermitian covariance matrix $\bm{S}_{ZZ}$ can be decomposed into the sum of the outer products of the eigenvectors $\bm{v}_i$ and the corresponding real-valued eigenvalues $\lambda_i$:

\begin{align}
    \bm{S}_{ZZ} = \sum_{i=1}^M \lambda_i \bm{v}_i \bm{v}_i^H.
\end{align}

Each eigenvalue $\lambda_i$ represents the amount of variance captured by the corresponding component, typically expressed as a fraction $\gamma_i$ of the total variance:

\begin{align}
    \gamma_i = \frac{\lambda_i}{\sum_{i=1}^M\lambda_i}
\end{align}

Projecting the data $\bm{Z}$ onto the $M$-dimensional subspace thus provides a lower-dimensional representation, called the \text{principal components} (PCs),

\begin{equation}\label{eq:theory:pca_projection}
    \bm{P} = \bm{Z} \bm{V},
\end{equation}

which also enables an approximate reconstruction of the original data from the projected  data:

\begin{align}\label{eq:theory:pca_reconstruction}
    \bm{Z} \approx \bm{P} \bm{V}^H.
\end{align}

This approximation is optimal in the least squares sense and becomes exact only when $M=\rank(X)$.

However, calculating the eigenvector decomposition is not always computationally efficient. A more efficient method involves using Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)~\cite[e.g.][p. 150]{horn_matrix_2012}, which decomposes the data matrix as

\begin{align}\label{eq:theory:pca_svd}
    \bm{Z} = \bm{U}' \bm{\Sigma} {\bm{V}'}^H,
\end{align}

where $\bm{U}'$ and $\bm{V}'$ are unitary matrices, ${\bm{U}'}^H \bm{U}' = {\bm{V}'}^H \bm{V}' = \bm{I}_M$, and $\bm{\Sigma}$ is a diagonal matrix containing the singular values $\sigma_i$ of $\bm{Z}$. The covariance matrix $\bm{S}_{ZZ}$ can then be expressed in terms of the SVD as

\begin{align}
    \bm{S} = \frac{1}{N-1} \bm{Z}^H \bm{Z} = \frac{1}{N-1} \bm{V}' \bm{\Sigma}^H \bm{U}'^H \bm{U}' \bm{\Sigma} \bm{V}'^H = \bm{V} \bm{\Lambda} {\bm{V}}^H,
\end{align}

where, by comparison with Equation \eqref{eq:theory:pca_eigenvector_decomposition} and \eqref{eq:theory:pca_projection}, $\bm{V} = \bm{V}'$, $\bm{\Lambda} = \frac{1}{N-1} \bm{\Sigma}^T \bm{\Sigma}$ and $\bm{P} = \bm{U}' \Sigma$. This establishes a direct relationship between the eigenvalues of the covariance matrix and the singular values of the data matrix:

\begin{align}
    \lambda_i = \frac{ \sigma_i^2}{N-1}.
\end{align}

Comparing \eqref{eq:theory:pca_reconstruction} with \eqref{eq:theory:pca_svd} shows that the PC scores $\bm{P}$ can be normalized

\begin{align}
    \bm{U} &\equiv \bm{U}'= \bm{P} \bm{\Sigma}^{-1} = \frac{1}{\ab(N-1)^{1/2}} \bm{P} \bm{\Lambda}^{-1/2}, \nonumber \\
    \text{or alternatively} \quad \bm{P} &= \ab(N-1)^{1/2} \bm{U} \bm{\Lambda}^{1/2}.
\end{align}

Thus, $\bm{U}^H\bm{U} = \bm{I}_M$ ensures that the PC scores $\bm{U}$ are unitary and uncorrelated.

A common scaling method for the vector of coefficients $\bm{V}$ involves scaling by the square root of the corresponding eigenvalues, referred to as \textbf{loadings}:

\begin{align}
    \tilde{\bm{V}} = \bm{V} \bm{\Lambda}^{1/2}.
\end{align}

Although not strictly unitary, these loadings form a generalized unitary matrix, as shown by

\begin{align}\label{eq:theory:pca_loadings_non_orthogonal}
    \tilde{\bm{V}}^H \tilde{\bm{V}} = \bm{\Lambda}.
\end{align}
Using this scaling, the Equation~\ref{eq:theory:pca_reconstruction} can be expressed as

\begin{align}\label{eq:theory:pca_reconstruction_loadings}
    \bm{X} = \ab(N-1)^{1/2} \bm{U} \tilde{\bm{V}}^T.
\end{align}

\paragraph{Weighting of Data Variables}

In some cases, it may be necessary to weight the data variables differently. This can be achieved by scaling the data matrix $\bm{Z}$ by a diagonal matrix $\bm{W}$, where the $j$\textsuperscript{th} diagonal element $w_j$ corresponds to the weight of the $j$\textsuperscript{th} variable. A very common situation is that the observed variables exhibit stark differences in their variance, which can sometimes lead to mask structure in the data as the PCA solution is solely driven by the different scales of variance (so the first principal axis will closely align to the variable with the highest variance) in which case the obvious choice is to use the inverse of the standard deviation of each variable, which ensures that all variables are equally weighted in which case the covariance matrix becomes the correlation matrix. Another common situation in climate science is that the physical fields are represented on regular longitude-latitude grids. This representation leads to a higher density of grid points near the poles, which typically results in an exaggerated importance of components spatially focused near the poles. To address this, the data matrix can be weighted by the square root of the cosine of the latitude \cite{north_latitude_1982}, $w_j = \sqrt{\cos(\text{lat}_j)}$, where $\text{lat}_j$ is the latitude of the $j$\textsuperscript{th} grid point. This weighting ensures that the grid points are equally weighted in the PCA solution, regardless of their geographical location. 

\paragraph{Computational Considerations}

The computational cost of performing eigendecomposition on the covariance matrix scales with $\order*{D^3}$ \cite[e.g.][Section 8.3]{golub_matrix_2013}, making it impractical for modern datasets, such as those in climate science, where typical data sizes are on the order of $D=\order*{10^6}$ or even larger. For example, ERA5 \cite{c3s_era5_hourly_single}, a widely used reference for state-of-the-art reanalysis climate data, distributes a single climate variable on a regular \ang{0.25} grid, resulting in $D=\num{1036800}$ grid cells.

In practice, more efficient algorithms, such as SVD or power methods~\cite{golub_matrix_2013}, have been traditionally employed. Recently, randomized low-rank approximation techniques~\cite{drineas_clustering_2004,halko_finding_2011,bogaardt_dataset_2019,erichson_sparse_2020} have further reduced computational complexity to $\order*{ND \log(M) + \ab(N+D)M^2}$. These advancements offer feasible alternatives for handling large, out-of-memory datasets (see Table~\ref{tab:pca_algorithm_complexity}).

\paragraph{Shortcomings} 

Despite its widespread use and versatility, PCA has several limitations that must be considered. First, PCA relies on linear transformations to reduce dimensionality by identifying principal components as linear combinations of the original variables (see Equation~\eqref{eq:theory:pca_projection}). This linearity assumption may not hold for complex systems—such as climate data—where variable relationships are often nonlinear~\cite[e.g.][]{hannachi_bimodality_2019}. In addition, the orthogonality imposed on the coefficient vectors is a mathematical convenience that may not reflect the physical reality, potentially leading to misinterpretation~\cite{richman_rotation_1986}. PCA's focus on directions of maximum variance can also cause it to overlook important but less variable features. Moreover, as a static method, it does not capture temporal dynamics~\cite{farrell_accurate_2001}, and it is sensitive to both outliers and scaling issues---extreme values can disproportionately influence the results, and variables with larger ranges may dominate unless properly standardized.

Over the past few decades, numerous modifications and extensions have been proposed to address these shortcomings, especially in the context of climate science. To relax the linearity assumption, variants based on non-parametric kernel methods~\cite{webb_approach_1996,scholkopf_nonlinear_1998,bueso_nonlinear_2020} or neural networks~\cite{kramer_nonlinear_1991} have been developed. For improved interpretability, rotated~\cite{richman_rotation_1986} and sparse~\cite{jolliffe_simplified_2002} PCA techniques offer alternatives that better capture underlying physical processes. Robust PCA~\cite{candes_robust_2011} has emerged as a more resilient approach to outlier sensitivity, while extended~\cite{weare_examples_1982,broomhead_extracting_1986} and Hilbert~\cite{barnett_interaction_1983,horel_complex_1984} PCA methods have been introduced to account for temporal dynamics. Additionally, eigenmethods such as Optimal Persistence Analysis (OPA)~\cite{delsole_optimally_2001,delsole_low-frequency_2006} and Principal Oscillation Pattern (POP) analysis~\cite{hasselmann_pips_1988} provide alternative frameworks that do not rely solely on variance maximization to uncover dominant patterns.

In the following subsections, we explore these limitations and their corresponding solutions in greater detail. Specifically, we discuss how rotation of the PCA solution can enhance physical interpretability (Section~\ref{sec:theory:rotated_pca}) and how Hilbert-transforming the data offers insights into temporal evolution patterns (Section~\ref{sec:theory:hilbert_pca}).






\subsection{Sparse PCA via Rotation}\label{sec:theory:rotated_pca}
PCA is widely used for dimensionality reduction, as it maximizes the explained variance and provides orthogonality, making it optimal in a least-squares sense~\cite[][]{bishop_pattern_2006}. It effectively identifies principal axes that capture the covariance among variables, allowing for the examination of interrelationships between stations, grid points or variables. However, PCA is not ideal for identifying the cause of variation or deriving physical modes of variability~\cite{richman_rotation_1986,legates_effect_1993,jolliffe_principal_2002}. 

One limitation is that real-world processes do not necessarily adhere to the orthogonality imposed by PCA's eigenvectors. Additionally, the dense nature of these VCs -- where all elements are non-zero -- can make interpretation challenging. In the context of climate and environmental science, where variables are often represented across spatial grids, these limitations can lead to recognizable but artificial patterns known as Buell patterns~\cite{buell_physical_1979,richman_rotation_1986,legates_effect_1991}. These patterns typically start with a positive component and evolve into increasingly complex structures, such as bipole or tripole patterns (Figure~\ref{fig:theory:pca_buell_patterns}). Such issues highlight the disconnect between standard PCA and underlying physical processes~\cite{richman_rotation_1986}, prompting the development of alternative methods that produce sparse VCs, characterized by many zero or near-zero elements. This sparsity -- which necessarily implies the loss of orthogonality for at least one of the VCs and PC scores -- can enhance interpretability by localizing the patterns and reducing the density of the VCs.

In climate science, the most common approach to achieving sparse PCA is through rotation techniques~\cite{horel_rotated_1981,richman_obliquely_1981,richman_rotation_1986}, a concept borrowed from factor analysis \cite[cf.][pp. 269]{jolliffe_principal_2002}. In this "factor analysis viewpoint" of PCA \cite[][pp. 150-161]{jolliffe_principal_2002}, a linear transformation is applied to a subset of the VCs to derive a new set of rotated VCs that are "simpler" in a heuristically defined manner, ideally aligning better with the underlying physical processes. 

As noted in Equation~\eqref{eq:theory:pca_reconstruction_loadings}, once the orthogonality constraint is relaxed, PCA solutions exhibit inherent indeterminacy, allowing any non-singular transformation $\bm{R}$ to satisfy the PCA formulation \cite[e.g.][p. 305]{richman_rotation_1986}:

\begin{align}
    \bm{Z} = \ab(N-1)^{1/2} \bm{U} \tilde{\bm{V}}^H = \ab(N-1)^{1/2} \bm{U} \bm{R} \bm{R}^{-1} \tilde{\bm{V}}^H.
\end{align}

Rotated PCA exploits this indeterminacy by choosing $\bm{R}$ to optimize a simplicity criterion $f$, resulting in rotated loadings\footnote{In principle, it is possible to rotate the unitary matrix of coefficients $\bm{V}$ instead of the loadings matrix $\tilde{\bm{V}}$~\cite{mestasnunez_orthogonality_2000,jolliffe_principal_2002}. However, this approach is seldom used in practice. The primary reason is that rotating the unitary coefficient matrix preserves the orthogonality of the VCs but results in the loss of orthogonality among the PCs, which does not address issues like the Buell patterns. Moreover, this method does not consider the relative importance of the spatial patterns, as indicated by the eigenvalues, making the results of the rotation process highly sensitive to the selection of the number of components to rotate.} $\tilde{\bm{V}}_R = \tilde{\bm{V}} \bm{R}$ that maximize $f$:

\begin{align}
    \argmax_{\bm{R}} \, f(\tilde{\bm{V}}_R).
\end{align}


There are two main categories of rotation: \textit{orthogonal}\footnote{In the broader context of complex data, this would be referred to as a unitary rotation. However, since factor analysis has primarily been applied to real-valued data, the terms orthogonal and oblique rotations have become the standard. To avoid confusion, I use these historical terms while acknowledging that they also encompass the more general case of complex data.} and \textit{oblique}. In orthogonal rotation, $\bm{R}$ is a unitary matrix, preserving the uncorrelated nature of the PCs since $\bm{U}_R^H\bm{U}_R = \bm{R}^H\bm{U}^H\bm{U}\bm{R} = \bm{1}_M$. In oblique rotation, $\bm{R}$ is a general non-singular matrix, resulting in correlated PCs, as $\bm{U}_R^H\bm{U}_R = \bm{R}^H\bm{R}$.
Regardless of the method, the rotated loadings are not orthogonal,

\begin{align}
    \tilde{\bm{V}}_R^H \tilde{\bm{V}}_R = \bm{R}^H \tilde{\bm{V}}^H  \tilde{\bm{V}} \bm{R} \overset{\eqref{eq:theory:pca_loadings_non_orthogonal}}{=} \bm{R}^H \bm{\Lambda} \bm{R} \neq \bm{I}_M,
\end{align}

which is crucial for achieving a more interpretable solution. Various simplicity criteria $f$ have been proposed~\cite[see e.g.][]{richman_rotation_1986}, with most focusing on making the rotated loadings more localized and physically interpretable. In practice, the specific choice of simplicity criterion often has little impact on the results~\cite{richman_rotation_1986}.

\paragraph{Varimax Orthogonal Rotation} The most widely used orthogonal rotation technique is the varimax criterion~\cite{kaiser_varimax_1958}, which aims to simplify the VCs by maximizing the variance of the squared loadings,

\begin{align}
    f(\tilde{\bm{V}}_R) = \sum_{m=1}^{M} \ab(p \sum_{j=1}^{P} b_{jm}^2 - \ab(\sum_{j=1}^{P} b_{jm})^2 )
\end{align}

where $b_{jm}$ are the squared norm of the rotated loadings $|\tilde{v}_{R, jm}|^2 / h_j^2$ normalized by the \textit{communalities} $h_j^2 = \sum_{m=1}^{M}|\tilde{v}_{R, jm}|^2$ of variable $j$. The varimax rotation pushes the elements of $\tilde{\bm{V}}_R$ towards either 0 or 1, creating a sparse solution with primarily large or small values, which is particularly useful for identifying localized physical processes, such as atmospheric or oceanic teleconnections.

\paragraph{Promax Oblique Rotation} The promax criterion~\cite{hendrickson_promax_1964}, a generalization of the varimax criterion, is one of the most common oblique rotation techniques. It is based on the premise that an orthogonal simple structure solution, such as varimax, is often close to an optimal oblique solution. The promax method constructs a hypothetical oblique target matrix $\bm{T}$ by performing an oblique Procrustes transformation~\cite{hurley_procrustes_1962} on the varimax-rotated loadings matrix,

\begin{align}\label{eq:theory:procrustes}
    \bm{T} = \tilde{\bm{V}}_R \bm{R} + \bm{E},
\end{align}

where $\bm{R}$ is the oblique rotation matrix and $\bm{E}$ is a matrix of residuals. The normal equation for solving the complex Procrustes problem is given by:

\begin{align}
    \bm{R} = (\tilde{\bm{V}}_R^H \tilde{\bm{V}}_R)^{-1} \bm{T}^H \tilde{\bm{V}}_R.
\end{align}

To define the target matrix $T$, the promax criterion increases the discrimination between high and low loadings by raising the loadings to a power $q$ while retaining the original sign,

\begin{align}
    t_{jm} = \frac{\ab|a_{jm}|^{q+1}}{a_{jm}},
\end{align}

where $q$ controls the degree of obliqueness and $a_{jm}$ are the elements of the max-normalized, varimax-rotated loadings matrix $\bm{A}$, defined as 

\begin{align}
    \bm{A} &= \tilde{\bm{A}} \bm{K}^{-1}, \\
    \tilde{\bm{A}} &= \bm{H}^{-1}\tilde{\bm{V}}_R,
\end{align}

with $\bm{K}$ being a diagonal matrix containing the maximum absolute values of each column of $\tilde{\bm{A}}$, $a_{m} = \max_{m} \ab|A_{pm}|$, and $\bm{H}$ a diagonal matrix with the square root of the communalities $h_j$ for the $j$\textsuperscript{th} variable.

\paragraph{Application and Criticism} Both orthogonal and oblique rotation techniques, particularly varimax and promax, have been extensively used since the 1980s to identify stationary modes of variability in climate data~\cite[e.g.][]{horel_rotated_1981,barnston_classification_1987,lim_east_2015,gao_impacts_2017,mezzina_dynamics_2020,huth_how_2021,lee_quantifying_2019}. Notable climate oscillations, such as the North Atlantic Oscillation (NAO),
have been defined based on varimax-rotated patterns of loadings~\cite{barnston_classification_1987}. These techniques also help identify "ghost patterns", artificial patterns identified by PCA that are not physically meaningful~\cite{lian_evaluation_2012,huth_how_2021}.

Rotated modes offer several advantages, such as greater robustness against temporal and spatial subsampling~\cite{cheng_robustness_1995} compared to unrotated modes. They also effectively mitigate the statistical artifacts associated with Buell patterns, resulting in more realistic representations of the underlying autocorrelation structure and thus increased interpretability~\cite{richman_climatic_1985,richman_rotation_1986,lian_evaluation_2012}. Moreover, rotation techniques have been successfully integrated into other PCA extensions dealing with the non-linear and non-stationary nature of climate oscillations~\cite{horel_complex_1984,bueso_nonlinear_2020,guilloteau_rotated_2020}.

However, rotation techniques are not without criticism, mainly due to the ad-hoc nature of the various simplicity criteria~\cite[cf.][pp. 270-274]{jolliffe_principal_2002}. This has led to the exploration of alternative approaches to enforce sparsity. Notably, \citet{jolliffe_simplified_2002} introduced the Simplified Component Technique - LASSO (SCoTLASS), a sparse PCA variant based on the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO), originally from regularized regression \cite{tibshirani_regression_1996}. This method imposes a constraint on the $\ell_1$ norm of the vector of loadings, resulting in some loading coefficients being exactly zero.

Despite its advantages, SCoTLASS has drawbacks, particularly when the number of variables $D$ is much larger than the number of observations $N$. In such cases, SCoTLASS can only select at most $N$ non-zero elements in the loading vectors, which is not ideal in climate science where often $D \gg N$. Additionally, since SCoTLASS solutions cannot be obtained via SVD and require a numerical approach using projected gradient methods~\cite{trendafilov_projected_2006}, the computational demand is typically higher 
than for (rotated) PCA. While this method has inspired many other sparse PCA techniques based on additional constraints (usually the $1$-norm or $2$-norm)~\cite[see e.g.][]{zou_sparse_2006,zou_selective_2018,erichson_sparse_2020}, these formulations are often complex and require algorithmic innovations to avoid issues like local optima and ensure convergence. They may also face challenges with memory limitations, slow computation speeds and limited effectiveness in modelling the data~\cite{guerra-urzola_guide_2021,camacho_all_2020}.

It is likely for these reasons that rotated PCA remains one of the most commonly used techniques for achieving sparse PCA solutions in climate science. It is computationally efficient, straightforward to implement and provides a more interpretable understanding of the data compared to standard PCA.

\subsection{Hilbert PCA}\label{sec:theory:hilbert_pca}
While PCA efficiently identifies the principal axes along which the covariance among all variables is distributed, and rotated PCA provides a more sparse solution related to underlying physical processes, both methods are inherently static. They do not account for temporal dynamics or changes over time, as they only consider the current state of the physical field. This limitation is significant in climate science, where time evolution is crucial. For instance, standard PCA does not differentiate between the order of data points over time, resulting in the same eigenvectors and eigenvalues regardless of temporal permutations. 

This static nature poses a challenge for capturing oscillatory patterns, such as the quasi-biennial oscillation (QBO), Madden-Julian oscillation (MJO) \cite{madden_detection_1971} and ENSO, which involve spatial patterns propagating over time. These phenomena cannot be adequately captured by standard PCA.

\subsubsection{Hilbert Transform as a Momentum Operator}

To address this limitation, one approach is to model both the current state of the climate and its tendency or `momentum', analogous to the Hamiltonian principle in mechanics, where a system's future is described by first-order differential equations for location (state) and momentum (tendency)~\cite[cf.][p. 353]{storch_statistical_1999}. In classical mechanics, propagating features are represented using canonical coordinates: each state variable is augmented by a momentum variable, allowing oscillations to be depicted as rotations in a two-dimensional phase space. From a data perspective, this augmentation can be achieved via the Hilbert transform ($\mathcal{H}$). For a real time series $x(t)$, the Hilbert transform $x'(t)$ is defined as


\begin{align}\label{eq:theory:hilbert_transform_time}
x'(t) \equiv \HT{x}(t) = \frac{1}{\pi} \pv \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{x(\tau)}{\tau - t} d\tau,
\end{align}

where $\pv$ denotes the Cauchy principal value ensuring convergence despite the singularity at $\tau=t$. The Hilbert transform emphasizes the local properties of $x(t)$ by convolution with $\frac{1}{\pi t}$. In the frequency domain, it is related to the Fourier transform $\mathcal{F}$ by

\begin{align}\label{eq:theory:hilbert_transform_frequency}
    \FT{x'}(\omega) = -i \sgn (\omega) \FT{x}(\omega)
\end{align}

where the imaginary unit $i$ indicates a phase shift of $\pi/2$ for the frequency components $\omega$ of $x$, placing $x'$ in quadrature to $x$. This provides an intuitive interpretation of the Hilbert transform, as it acts as a linear filter that alters the phase of the signal's components by 90 degrees without changing their amplitude.

By using the Hilbert transform, a real-valued time series $x(t)$ can be complexified into $z(t) = x(t) + i \HT{x}(t)$, known as the \textit{analytic signal}, which allows for the computation of the instantaneous amplitude and phase of $x(t)$:

\begin{align}
    A(t) = |z(t)| = \sqrt{x(t)^2 + \HT{x}(t)^2}, \quad \phi(t) = \arg(z(t)) = \arctan \left( \frac{\HT{x}(t)}{x(t)} \right).
\end{align}

Similar results can be obtained for the discrete case~\cite[][pp. 942-956]{oppenheim_discrete-time_2010}, and this concept can be extended to multivariate data~\cite[e.g.][pp. 105-106]{hannachi_patterns_2021}, 
defining the analytic signal of a data matrix $X$ as

\begin{align}
    \bm{Z} = \bm{X} + i \HT{\bm{X}},
\end{align}

where $\HT{\bm{X}}$ is the feature-wise Hilbert transform of $\bm{X}$. 


\citet{rasmusson_biennial_1981} introduced the concept of Hilbert PCA using this data augmentation technique\footnote{Although \citet{rasmusson_biennial_1981} originally termed it \textit{Hilbert Singular Decomposition}, the terms \textit{Hilbert EOF/PCA} and \textit{Complex EOF} anaylsis are more commonly used in the literature \cite{jolliffe_principal_2002,storch_statistical_1999,hannachi_patterns_2021,burger_intraseasonal_2021}.     In this thesis, we adopt the term Hilbert PCA to align with standard conventions and to distinguish it from Complex PCA, a different method that does not involve the Hilbert transform. Complex PCA constructs a complex signal by combining two separate variables---often representing zonal and meridional flow, such as wind speed---as the real and imaginary components before applying complex SVD.}. This method, refined by \citet{barnett_interaction_1983} and \citet{horel_complex_1984}, involves applying PCA to the augmented data matrix $\bm{Z}$. By incorporating phase information, Hilbert PCA can capture the direction and speed of wave-like patterns, unlike traditional PCA, which only identifies standing oscillations. The resulting eigenvectors and principal component scores are complex, with the real and imaginary components linked via the Hilbert transform. The variance captured by each component is represented by the real eigenvalues of the complex covariance matrix of $\bm{Z}$ (see Section~\ref{sec:theory:pca}).

The main advantage of Hilbert PCA is the ability to identify and reconstruct propagating waves, which is especially valuable in studying climate phenomena characterized by such patterns, including MJO, ENSO and QBO. For instance, \citet{cai_forcing_2001} utilized Hilbert PCA to analyze SST in the Southern Hemisphere, linking the findings to ENSO, while \citet{burger_intraseasonal_2021} employed it to develop an index for the Boreal summer intraseasonal oscillation (BSISO). However, to reliably identify oscillatory patterns, sufficiently long time series are required to resolve at least one full oscillation~\cite{merrifield_detecting_1990}. Additionally, interpreting of the Hilbert transform as representing the instantaneous frequency is valid holds for narrowband signals; the phase of the Hilbert transform is not well-defined for broadband signals~\cite{gabor_theory_1946,boashash_estimating_1992}. Moreover, real-time monitoring is challenging with Hilbert PCA because new real-valued data cannot be directly projected onto the existing patterns without knowing or estimating the exact momentum (phase) of the new data, which requires future information (Equation~\eqref{eq:theory:hilbert_transform_time}). To mitigate this, an approximate real-time (causal) Hilbert transform can be employed~\cite{burger_complex_1993,burger_intraseasonal_2021}, offering a practical remedy to the issue of real-time applicability. Finally, Hilbert PCA shares PCA's limitations regarding physical interpretability due to the orthogonality of the VCs. To address this, rotated Hilbert PCA has been proposed to enhance interpretability~\cite{horel_rotated_1981,bloomfield_orthogonal_1994}. 


\section{Maximum Covariance Analysis}
\label{sec:theory:mca}

In contrast to finding latent variables that maximize variance within a single dataset, Maximum Covariance Analysis (MCA) aims to identify the latent variables that are common in two sets of data and maximize covariance. For example, in the case of the MJO \cite{madden_detection_1971}, which is the dominant mode of intraseasonal variability in the tropical atmosphere, characterized by an eastward-moving cell of overturning wind circulation and associated regions of enhanced and suppressed precipitation, MCA allows us to focus on shared variability between different variables while ignoring dominant variations associated with only one variable, such as diurnal cycles linked to surface processes absent at higher atmosphere levels. 
MCA gained popularity in the field of meteorology\footnote{According to \citet[][p. 225]{jolliffe_principal_2002}, the technique was first used in psychology in the 1950s under the name \textit{inter-battery factor analysis}. As a variant of Partial Least Squares (PLS), it was later applied in biomedical sciences by \citet{sampson_neurobehavioral_1989} and was referred to as \textit{PLS-SVD} by \citet{wegelin_survey_2000}. In meteorology, \citet{bretherton_intercomparison_1992} introduced the term \textit{SVD analysis}, which remains in use today \cite[e.g.][]{mori_reconciled_2019}. Occasionally, the method is also called \textit{Canonical Covariance Analysis}, similar to Canonical Correlation Analysis \cite[e.g.][]{cherry_singular_1996,hannachi_patterns_2021}.} through the works of \citet{bretherton_intercomparison_1992} and \citet{wallace_singular_1992}, since when it has been extensively applied \cite[e.g.][]{wallace_singular_1992,tsai_maximum_2009,li_north_2016,barreto_maximum_2017,mori_reconciled_2019,rathore_improving_2021}.

MCA identifies patterns of maximum covariance between datasets by finding pairs of linear subspaces\footnote{\citet{cherry_comments_1997} provided an interesting, complementary view: MCA can be seen as two PCAs followed by rotation to align the results of the two analyses as closely as possible. Based on this view, it becomes immediately clear that MCA reduces to PCA when both sets are identical.} with high covariance \cite[e.g.][p. 321]{storch_statistical_1999}. Unlike PCA, which operates on a single set of variables, MCA considers two\footnote{In theory, this can be extended to more than two sets of data \cite{kettenring_canonical_1971}.} sets of variables and seeks components that exhibit high covariance across datasets, sometimes called cross-covariance. This approach can be formulated as an optimization problem, aiming to find pairs of (complex-valued) coupled patterns $\bm{v}_{s,1}$ for datasets $\bm{Z}_s$, $s=\{x, y\}$, such that the projection maximizes the covariance between the datasets:

\begin{align}\label{eq:theory:mca_maximization}
    \argmax_{\bm{v}_{x,1}, \bm{v}_{y,1}} \, \bm{v}_{x,1}^H \bm{C}_{xy} \bm{v}_{y,1} \quad \text{subject to} \quad \Vab{\bm{v}_{x,1}}_2 = \Vab{\bm{v}_{y,1}}_2 = 1,
\end{align}

where the cross-covariance matrix between the two datasets is given by

\begin{align}\label{eq:theory:mca_cross_covariance}
    \bm{C}_{xy} = \frac{1}{N-1} \bm{Z}_x^H \bm{Z}_y.
\end{align}

The solution to this problem gives rise to a generalized eigenvalue equations similar to that of PCA, which can also be solved by using the SVD of the cross-covariance matrix,

\begin{align}\label{eq:theory:mca_svd}
    \bm{C}_{xy} = \bm{V}_x \bm{\Sigma} \bm{V}_y^H,
\end{align}

% Proof that optimality of leading SVD components: https://atmos.washington.edu/~breth/classes/AS552/lect/lect22.pdf

where the $k$\textsuperscript{th} column of the left and right singular vectors, $\bm{V}_x$ and $\bm{V}_y$, contains the pattern vectors $\bm{v}_{x,k}$ and $\bm{v}_{y,k}$, and $\bm{\Sigma}$ a diagonal matrix containing the singular values $\sigma_k$ in descending order, with $k=1\dots M$ and $M=\rank(\bm{C}_{xy})$. The first left and right singular vectors correspond to the maximum covariance, given by the largest singular value $\sigma_1$.

The associated time series of these pattern vectors, typically called expansion coefficients, $\bm{P}_s$ for each dataset are obtained by projecting the data onto the pattern vectors:

\begin{align}\label{eq:theory:mca_projection}
    \bm{P}_s &= \bm{Z}_s \bm{V}_s,
\end{align}

with the expansion coefficients $\bm{P}_s$, $s\in \{x, y\}$. Note that in MCA an exact reconstruction of the original data is in general not possible, in contrast to PCA (see Equation \eqref{eq:theory:pca_reconstruction}), as the cross-covariance matrix has in general lower rank than the original data matrices, i.e. $\rank(\bm{C}_{xy}) \leq \min(M_x, M_y)$, where $M_x$ and $M_y$ are the number of variables in each dataset. In other words, the singular vectors $\bm{V}_x$ and $\bm{V}_y$ span a subspace of the original data space, capturing the shared variability between the two datasets, and thus--in general--do not provide a complete basis for either $\bm{Z}_x$ or $\bm{Z}_y$. Equation~\eqref{eq:theory:mca_projection} can be used though to reconstruct the shared variability into the original data space of each dataset:

\begin{align}\label{eq:theory:mca_reconstruction}
    \bm{\hat{Z}}_s \approx \bm{P}_s \bm{V}_s^H.
\end{align}


It can be shown that the expansion coefficients $\bm{P}_x$ and $\bm{P}_y$ are jointly orthogonal:

\begin{align}\label{eq:theory:mca_covariance}
    \frac{1}{N-1}\bm{P}_x^H \bm{P}_y \overset{\eqref{eq:theory:mca_projection}}&{=} \frac{1}{N-1} \bm{V}_x^H \bm{Z}_x^H \bm{Z}_y \bm{V}_y \overset{\eqref{eq:theory:mca_cross_covariance}}{=} \bm{V}_x^H \bm{C}_{xy} \bm{V}_y \overset{\eqref{eq:theory:mca_svd}}{=} \bm{\Sigma}.
\end{align}

However, the expansion coefficients $\bm{P}_x$ and $\bm{P}_y$ are not orthogonal within themselves, meaning their covariance matrices $\bm{P}_s^H\bm{P}_s$ are generally not diagonal. 

Equation~\eqref{eq:theory:mca_covariance} can also be interpreted as the total covariance between $\bm{X}$ and $\bm{Y}$, given by the trace of $\bm{\Sigma}$. Therefore, one may use the singular values to assess the relative importance of each mode. However, this total covariance is \emph{not} equal to the sum of covariances between $\bm{X}$ and $\bm{Y}$ before decomposition. The reason is that covariances can be positive or negative, and summing them directly may result in cancellations. Moreover, the singular values $\sigma_k$ are always non-negative, representing the magnitude of covariance captured by each mode, without accounting for sign. That is, the total covariance is \emph{not invariant} before and after decomposition. 

A more appropriate measure to assess the relative importance of each mode in capturing the shared variability between the two datasets is the \emph{squared} covariance. The reason for this can be seen by computing the squared Frobenius norm of the cross-covariance matrix,

\begin{align}\label{eq:theory:total_squared_covariance}
    \Vab{\bm{C}_{xy}}_F^2 &\equiv  \pab{\sum_i^N \sum_j^P \vab{c_{ij}}^2} = \sum_{i}^{M} \sigma_i^2,
\end{align}

where $M$ is the rank of the cross-covariance matrix. The result shows that the total squared covariance of the cross-covariance matrix $\bm{C}_{xy}$ is an invariant and the same as the sum of the squared singular values. A formal proof for this statement is provided in Appendix~\ref{app:proof:frobenius_norm}. Therefore, the relative importance of mode $k$ can be quantified by the squared covariance fraction (SCF) $f_{xy, k}$:

\begin{align}\label{eq:theory:mca_scf_singular_values}
    f_{xy, k} = \frac{\sigma_k^2}{\sum_{i=1}^{M} \sigma_i^2}.
\end{align}

Alternatively, this expression can be formulated as a residual-based measure \cite{swenson_continuum_2015}:

\begin{align}\label{eq:theory:mca_scf_residual}
    f_{xy, k} = 1 - \frac{\Vab{(\bm{Z}_{x} - \bm{\hat{Z}}_{x,k})^H (\bm{Z}_{y} - \bm{\hat{Z}}_{y,k})}_F^2}{\Vab{\bm{Z}_{x}^H\bm{Z}_{y}}_F^2}
\end{align}

where $\bm{\hat{Z}}_{x,k}$ and $\bm{\hat{Z}}_{y,k}$ represent the shared variability of the $k$\textsuperscript{th} mode reconstructed in the original data space using Equation~\eqref{eq:theory:mca_reconstruction}. This fraction provides a measure of how much of the shared variability between the two datasets is captured by the $k$\textsuperscript{th} mode. 

To assess the relative importance of the patterns in relation to each dataset, it is necessary to consider the variance within the individual datasets explained by each mode relative to the total variance of the individual datasets. This can be expressed using the squared Frobenius norm of the individual datasets:

\begin{align}
    f_{ss, k} &= \frac{\bm{v}_{s,k}^H\bm{C}_{ss}\bm{v}_{s,k}}{\tr \left(\bm{Z}_s\right)} = \frac{\bm{p}_{s,k}^H\bm{p}_{s,k}}{\Vab{\bm{Z}_s}_F^2}.
\end{align}

Note that this projection-based measure of explained variance is mathematically the same in MCA as the following residual-based measure:

\begin{align}\label{eq:theory:mca_explained_variance}
    f_{ss, k} &= 1 - \frac{\Vab{\bm{Z}_s - \bm{\hat{Z}}_{s,k}^2}}{\Vab{\bm{Z}_s}_F^2}
\end{align}

While the SCF $f_{xy, k}$ indicates the amount of shared variability between the two datasets, it does not provide information about the strength of the association or coupling between them. To measure this, we use the correlation coefficient $\rho_k$ for the expansion coefficients of mode $k$ between the two datasets:

\begin{align}
    \rho_k = \frac{\cov (\bm{p}_{x, k}, \bm{p}_{y, k}) }{\pab{\var(\bm{p}_{x, k}) \var(\bm{p}_{y, k})}^{1/2}} =  \frac{\sigma_k}{\Vab{\bm{p}_{x, k}}^2 \Vab{\bm{p}_{y, k}}^2}.
\end{align}

The homogeneous covariance patterns \cite{bretherton_intercomparison_1992} for the pattern vectors are:

\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
        \bm{h}_{M,x,k} = \cov(\bm{Z}_x, \bm{p}_{x, k}) = \bm{S}_{xx} \bm{v}_{x, k},\\
        \bm{h}_{M,y,k} = \cov(\bm{Z}_y, \bm{p}_{y, k}) = \bm{S}_{yy} \bm{v}_{y, k},
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}

These patterns provide an indicator of which variables or features are important for the co-varying components in both fields. The corresponding heterogeneous covariance patterns are defined as:

\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}\label{eq:theory:mca_heterogeneous_patterns}
        \bm{h}_{T,x,k} = \cov(\bm{Z}_x, \bm{p}_{y, k}) = \sigma_i \bm{v}_{y, k},\\
        \bm{h}_{T,y,k} = \cov(\bm{Z}_y, \bm{p}_{x, k}) = \sigma_i \bm{v}_{x, k}.
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}

These patterns indicate how well the variables in one field can be predicted from the $k$\textsuperscript{th} expansion coefficient of the other field. One may also use standardized data to obtain the respective homogeneous and heterogeneous correlation patterns.


\paragraph{Computational Complexity} In MCA, computing the cross-covariance matrix (see~\eqref{eq:theory:mca_cross_covariance}) can be a significant bottleneck, as its dimensions depend on the number of grid points or, more generally, the feature dimensions to be reduced. For instance, modern climate datasets, such as ERA5, comprise on the order of one million grid points for a single variable at one pressure level. Consequently, storing this matrix may require between 1 and 10 terabytes of memory, depending on the numerical precision (e.g., \texttt{np.float32} versus \texttt{np.float64} in Python). These substantial memory demands, combined with the computational  cost of forming the matrix and performing its SVD, render the direct approach impractical in many applications. A viable alternative is to perform MCA in a reduced subspace spanned by the principal components of each dataset. When combined with randomized linear algebra techniques \cite{halko_finding_2011}, this PCA preprocessing step yields significant computational gains, particularly for moderate to large datasets.

\subsection{Rotated Hilbert MCA}\label{sec:theory:mca_rotated_hilbert}
The method's orthogonal patterns ensure efficient dimensionality reduction of the covariance but limit physical interpretability, similar to PCA. To enhance interpretability, rotation of the MCA solution can be employed. In the following, we outline the rotation procedure as proposed by \citet{cheng_orthogonal_1995}.

A `loaded' composite matrix $L$ of the left and right singular vectors truncated to the first $R$ modes, $\bm{\tilde{V}}_{s}$, is constructed and a simplicity criterion (e.g. varimax) is applied,

\begin{align}
    \bm{L} = 
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \bm{\tilde{V}}_{x}\\
        \bm{\tilde{V}}_{y}
    \end{bmatrix}
    \Sigma^{1/2},
\end{align}

where the term `loaded' refers to the fact that the truncated singular vectors are weighted by the square root of the singular values. The rotated pattern vectors $\bm{\tilde{V}}_{R,s}$ are then obtained by applying the rotation matrix $\bm{R}$ to the `loaded' $\bm{\tilde{V}}_{s}$:

\begin{align}\label{eq:theory:mca_rotated_pattern_vectors}
    \bm{\tilde{V}}_{R,s} =& \bm{\tilde{V}}_{s} \Sigma^{1/2} \bm{R} \bm{W}_s^{-1}
\end{align}

where $\bm{W}_s^{-1}$ is a diagonal matrix with the elements that normalize the rotated pattern vectors to unit length. Similarly, the associated rotated expansion coefficients $\bm{\tilde{P}}_{R,s}$ are then obtained by rotating the first $R$ non-rotated expansion coefficients $\bm{\tilde{P}}_{s}$:

\begin{align}
    \bm{\tilde{P}}_{R,x} =& \bm{\tilde{P}}_x \Sigma^{-1/2} \bm{R} \bm{W}_s
\end{align}

For an orthogonal rotation, the rotated expansion coefficients remain uncorrelated:

\begin{align}\label{eq:theory:mca_covariance_rotated_expansion_coefficients}
    \bm{\tilde{P}}_{R,x}^H \bm{\tilde{P}}_{R,y}  =& \pab{ \bm{\tilde{P}}_x \Sigma^{-1/2} \bm{R} \bm{W}_x}^H \pab{ \bm{\tilde{P}}_y \Sigma^{-1/2} \bm{R} \bm{W}_y} \nonumber\\
    =& \bm{W}_x \bm{R}^H \Sigma^{-1/2} \bm{\tilde{P}}_x^H \bm{\tilde{P}}_y \Sigma^{-1/2} \bm{R} \bm{W}_y \nonumber\\
    =& (N-1) \bm{W}_x \bm{W}_y.
\end{align}

while the unitary property of the rotated pattern vectors is removed:

\begin{align}
    \bm{\tilde{V}}_{R,x}^H \bm{\tilde{V}}_{R,y} =& \pab{ \bm{\tilde{V}}_x \Sigma^{1/2} \bm{R} W_x}^H \pab{ \bm{\tilde{V}}_y \Sigma^{1/2} \bm{R} \bm{W}_y^{-1}} \nonumber\\
    =& \bm{W}_x \bm{R}^H \Sigma^{1/2} \bm{\tilde{V}}_x^H \bm{\tilde{V}}_y \Sigma^{1/2} \bm{R} \bm{W}_y \nonumber\\
    =& \bm{W}_x \bm{R}^H \Sigma \bm{R} \bm{W}_y.
\end{align}

To assess the relative importance of the rotated modes, \citet{cheng_orthogonal_1995} proposed to use the fraction of covariance explained, defined as

\begin{align}\label{eq:theory:mca_rotated_covariance_fraction}
    f_{\text{CD}, k} = \frac{\sigma_k}{\sum_{i=1}^{M} \sigma_i}.
\end{align}

\citet{cheng_orthogonal_1995} argued that they chose this measure to be consistent with PCA and rotated PCA, although they acknowledge that it is the total squared covariance which is an invariant in MCA. 

In this thesis, we demonstrate that while the total covariance between two datasets is preserved under rotation, the total \emph{squared} covariance is not. To the best of our knowledge, this distinction has not been previously reported. This finding has practical implications for interpreting rotated MCA modes.

\subsubsection*{Total Squared Covariance Is Not Preserved Under Rotation}

Our goal is to verify whether the total squared covariance of the first $R$ rotated modes of datasets $\bm{Z}_x$ and $\bm{Z}_y$ equals that of the original modes obtained from MCA. We'll show that, except in trivial cases, it does not.

First, recall that the total squared covariance between $\bm{Z}_x$ and $\bm{Z}_y$ is given by the sum of the squared singular values $\sigma_k^2$ of their cross-covariance matrix $\bm{C}_{xy}$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:theory:total_squared_covariance}):

\begin{align}\label{eq:theory:mca_total_squared_covariance2}
    \Vab{\bm{C}_{xy}}_F^2 &= \sum_{k=1}^{M} \sigma_k^2.
\end{align}


Next, according to Equation~\eqref{eq:theory:mca_covariance_rotated_expansion_coefficients}, the covariance of the rotated expansion coefficients $\bm{\tilde{P}}_{R,x}$ and $\bm{\tilde{P}}_{R,y}$ is given by:

\begin{align}
    \frac{1}{N-1} \bm{\tilde{P}_{R,x}}^H \bm{\tilde{P}_{R,y}} &\overset{\eqref{eq:theory:mca_covariance_rotated_expansion_coefficients}}{=} \bm{W}_x \bm{W}_y.
\end{align}

To relate $\bm{W}_x$ and $\bm{W}_y$ to the original singular values $\sigma_k$, we use the normalization condition for the $k$\textsuperscript{th} rotated pattern vector $\bm{\tilde{V}}_{R,s,k}$, for $s\in \{x, y\}$:

\begin{align} | \bm{\tilde{V}}_{R,s,k} |^2 = 1. \end{align}

Using the expression for the rotated pattern vectors, we have:

\begin{align*}
    1 &= \Vab{\bm{\tilde{V}}_{R,s,k}}^2 \\
    &=\bm{\tilde{V}}_{R,s,k}^H \bm{\tilde{V}}_{R,s,k} \\
    \overset{\eqref{eq:theory:mca_rotated_pattern_vectors}}&{=} \pab{\bm{\tilde{V}}_s^H \bm{\Sigma}^{1/2} \bm{R}_{:,k} (\bm{W}_s^{-1})_{kk}}^H \pab{\bm{\tilde{V}}_s^H \bm{\Sigma}^{1/2} \bm{R}_{:,k} (\bm{W}_s^{-1})_{kk}} \\
    &= (\bm{W}_s^{-1})_{kk}^2 \pab{\bm{R}_{:,k}^H \bm{\Sigma} \bm{R}_{:,k}},
\end{align*}

where $\bm{R}_{:,k}$ is the $k$\textsuperscript{th} column of the rotation matrix $\bm{R}$.

Solving for $(\bm{W}_s)_{kk}$, we find:

\begin{align}
    (\bm{W}_s)_{kk}^2 &= \pab{\bm{R}_{:,k}^H \bm{\Sigma} \bm{R}_{:,k}} \implies \bm{W}_s^2 = \diag \pab{\bm{R}^H \bm{\Sigma} \bm{R}}.
\end{align}

This equation holds for both $\bm{W}_x$ and $\bm{W}_y$ implying that $\bm{W}_x = \bm{W}_y \equiv \bm{W}$. Therefore, the covariance between the rotated expansion coefficients, Equation~\eqref{eq:theory:mca_total_squared_covariance2}, simplifies to $\bm{W}^2$. Using the orthogonality of $\bm{R}$ and the fact that $\bm{\Sigma}$ is a diagonal matrix, we can find simplified expression for the diagonal elements of $\bm{W}^2$:

\begin{align}\label{eq:theory:mca_rotated_covariance_elements}
    \pab{\bm{W}^2}_{kk} &= \sum_{i=1}^{R} \bm{R}_{ik}^2 \sigma_i
\end{align}

For deriving the total squared covariance of the first $R$ rotated modes, we will make use of the Jensen inequality, which can be stated in its general, finite form as:

\begin{align}\label{eq:theory:jensen_inequality}
    f \pab{\frac{\sum_{i} a_i x_i}{\sum_{i} a_i}} \leq \frac{\sum_i a_i f(x_i)}{\sum_i a_i},
\end{align}

where $f$ is a real convex function and $a_i$ are positive weights. Note that equality holds if and only if $x_i = x_j$ for all $i$ and $j$ or if $f$ is a linear function. Specifically in our case, we define the convex, nonlinear function $f(x) \equiv x^2$ and the weights $a_i \equiv \bm{R}_{ik}^2$ and $x_i \equiv \sigma_i$, so that the Jensen inequality can be applied to the sum in Equation~\eqref{eq:theory:mca_rotated_covariance_elements}:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:theory:jensen_inequality_applied}
    \pab{\pab{\bm{W}^2}_{kk}}^2 = f \pab{\sum_{i=1}^{R} \bm{R}_{ik}^2 \sigma_i} = f \pab{\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{R} \bm{R}_{ik}^2 \sigma_i}{\sum_{i=1}^R \bm{R}_{ik}^2}}  < \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{R} \bm{R}_{ik}^2 \sigma_i^2}{\sum_{i=1}^R \bm{R}_{ik}^2}  = \sum_{i=1}^{R} \bm{R}_{ik}^2 \sigma_i^2.
\end{equation}

where we used the fact that $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal, i.e. $\sum_{i=1}^R \bm{R}_{ik}^2 = 1$. Also, the inequality is strict because function $f$ is nonlinear, and, we can safely assume that $\sigma_i \neq \sigma_j$ for $i \neq j$, as well as that the rotation matrix $\bm{R}$ is not the identity matrix.

The total squared covariance of the first $R$ rotated modes is then:

\begin{align}
    \tr{\bm{W}^4} &= \sum_{k=1}^{R} \pab{\bm{W}^2}_{kk}^2 \nonumber\\
    \overset{\eqref{eq:theory:mca_rotated_covariance_elements}}&{=} \sum_{k=1}^{R} \pab{\sum_{i=1}^{R} \bm{R}_{ik}^2 \sigma_i}^2 \nonumber\\
    \overset{\eqref{eq:theory:jensen_inequality_applied}}&{\leq} \sum_{k=1}^{R} \pab{\sum_{i=1}^{R} \bm{R}_{ik}^2 \sigma_i^2} \nonumber\\
    &= \sum_{i=1}^{R} \sigma_i^2 \sum_{k=1}^{R} \bm{R}_{ik}^2 \nonumber\\
    &= \sum_{i=1}^R \sigma_i^2.
\end{align}

The final result shows that the total squared covariance of the first $R$ rotated modes is less than the total squared covariance of the original modes. This shows that the total squared covariance after rotation is generally less than before rotation, unless the rotation matrix $\bm{R}$ is the identity matrix. Thus, total squared covariance is not preserved under rotation.

\subsubsection*{Total Covariance Is Preserved Under Rotation}

In contrast, the total covariance (without squaring) remains unchanged under rotation. We can show this following a similar approach to above:

\begin{align}
    \tr{\bm{W}^2} &= \sum_{k=1}^{R} \sum_{i=1}^{R} \bm{R}_{ik}^2 \sigma_i \nonumber\\
    &= \sum_{i=1}^{R} \sigma_i \sum_{k=1}^{R} \bm{R}_{ik}^2 \nonumber\\
    &= \sum_{i=1}^R \sigma_i.
\end{align}

The implication is that Equation~\eqref{eq:theory:mca_rotated_covariance_fraction} can be used to assess the relative importance of each rotated mode. However, it is important to interpret this with caution. While total covariance remains invariant before and after rotation, it does not hold as an invariant for MCA itself. Therefore, Equation~\eqref{eq:theory:mca_rotated_covariance_fraction} should be viewed as a general measure of the relative importance of the rotated modes rather than the amount of explained covariance between $\bm{Z}_x$ and $\bm{Z}_y$.

Unfortunately, because total squared covariance is not preserved under rotation, we encounter a limitation. In MCA, total squared covariance is an invariant, but this invariance does not hold after rotation. Conversely, total covariance is preserved under rotation but is not an invariant within MCA. For the practical applications presented in Chapter~\ref{chapter:tele}, we choose to use the SCF as a measure of relative importance, acknowledging that it does not sum to one after rotation.

Finally, before applying rotated MCA to real-world data, we must make two key decisions: (1) determining the optimal number of modes to rotate, and (2) choosing between orthogonal or oblique rotation.

\subsubsection*{Determining the Number of Modes to Rotate}\label{sec:theory:mode_stability_index}

Choosing the optimal number of modes to rotate is crucial because it can significantly affect the final rotated solution \cite[cf.][p.~271]{jolliffe_principal_2002}. In standard PCA, numerous methods exist—such as the scree plot, cumulative explained variance, North's rule-of-thumb \cite{north_sampling_1982}, and bootstrapping approaches \cite[e.g.][]{preisendorfer_principal_1988,jolliffe_principal_2002}—to guide this choice. Moreover, recent studies have derived optimal hard thresholds for singular values under specific noise assumptions to distinguish signal from noise \cite{gavish_optimal_2014,donoho_screenot_2021}. In rotated PCA, \citet{bueso_nonlinear_2020} even proposed a kurtosis-based measure for mode selection.

For rotated MCA, \citet{cheng_orthogonal_1995} argued that the rotated patterns should remain stable over a wide range of truncation points. However, their selection was ultimately subjective. Here, we propose an objective alternative: the \emph{mode stability index} $\kappa$. This index quantitatively assesses the consistency of mode patterns and provides a robust criterion for selecting the optimal number of modes to retain and rotate.

The underlying idea is simple: if the rotation solution is stable, the leading mode patterns should remain consistent even as the number of rotated modes varies within a reasonable range \cite{cheng_orthogonal_1995}. Once the essential structures are captured, adding extra modes should not significantly change the pattern.

To compute the mode stability index, we proceed as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Generate multiple rotation solutions:} Rotate the first $R$ modes over a range $R\in[R_{\text{min}}, R_{\text{max}}]$ to capture a variety of solutions. The range $[1, R_{\text{min}}]$ defines the set of modes whose stability is assessed.
    \item \textbf{Extract pattern vectors:} For each rotation, extract the amplitude pattern vectors $\vab{\bm{v}{k}^R}$ for the leading modes, where $k\in[1, R]$. 
    \item \textbf{Measure pairwise similarity:} Compare the amplitude patterns of mode $k$ across different rotation solutions using cosine similarity: 
    \begin{align}
        c{k, k'}^{R, R'} = \frac{\vab{\bm{v}k^R}^T \vab{\bm{v}{k'}^{R'}}}{\Vab{\bm{v}{k}^{R}} \cdot \Vab{\bm{v}{k'}^{R'}}}.
    \end{align}
    Cosine similarity ranges from \num{-1} (exact opposites) to \num{1} (identical), with \num{0} indicating orthogonal patterns. 
    \item \textbf{Determine mode similarity:} For each mode $k$ in solution $R$, identify the most similar mode in solution $R'$ by maximizing the cosine similarity over all leading modes:
    \begin{align}
        C_{k}^{R, R'} = \max_{k'\in [1, R_{\text{min}}]} \left( c_{k, k'}^{R, R'} \right).
    \end{align}
    This maximization accounts for possible reordering of modes between solutions. 
    \item \textbf{Define a consistency indicator:} Introduce an indicator function $\delta_{k}^{R, R'}$ that is 1 if $C_{k}^{R, R'}$ exceeds a chosen threshold $\tau$ (e.g. \num{0.9}) and 0 otherwise: \begin{align} 
        \delta_{k}^{R, R'} = \begin{cases} 
            1, & \text{if } C_{k}^{R, R'} > \tau,\\[1mm]
            0, & \text{otherwise}.
        \end{cases}
    \end{align} 
    \item \textbf{Compute the mode stability index:} Finally, average the indicator function over all pairs of solutions and modes: 
    \begin{align}\label{eq:theory:mode_stability_index}
        \kappa^R = \frac{1}{R_{\text{min}} \pab{R_{\text{max}} - R_{\text{min}}}} \sum_{R'=R_{\text{min}}}^{R_{\text{max}}} \sum_{k=1}^{R_{\text{min}}} \delta_{k}^{R, R'}.
    \end{align}
\end{enumerate}

The mode stability index $\kappa$ represents the average fraction of modes that remain consistent across different rotation solutions. A $\kappa$ value close to 1 indicates high stability---meaning the patterns are robust to changes in the number of rotated modes---whereas a low $\kappa$ suggests sensitivity to the selected number, implying potential instability or noise influence. Plotting $\kappa$ against the number of modes rotated can thus reveal the point where adding more modes no longer improves stability, guiding the optimal choice.

\subsubsection*{Selecting Between Orthogonal and Oblique Rotation} 
Choosing between orthogonal and oblique rotation involves balancing interpretability with complexity. Orthogonal rotations keep the expansion coefficients uncorrelated, while producing more localized spatial patterns, which may simplify their interpretation. However, the lack of correlation between coefficients may not accurately reflect the interconnected nature of geophysical processes. In contrast, oblique rotations allow the expansion coefficients to be correlated, potentially capturing more realistic relationships, while still producing simpler spatial structures. This added realism comes at the cost of increased complexity in interpretation.

In this thesis, we compared the outcomes of both orthogonal and oblique rotations to evaluate the degree of correlation between the expansion coefficients. When the correlation between leading modes was weak (for example, an absolute correlation coefficient below $0.2$), we opted for orthogonal rotation to maintain simplicity and clarity in the results.


\subsubsection*{Hilbert MCA}
Beyond rotation for better interpretability, Hilbert MCA can be applied to capture the temporal evolution of coupled patterns, akin to Hilbert PCA (see Section~\ref{sec:theory:hilbert_pca}). This is especially useful for analyzing propagating patterns like the MJO, which involve covarying patterns in different climate variables, such as wind speed in the troposphere and lower stratosphere, and precipitation. By augmenting data with the Hilbert transform, we obtain complexified fields and solve the maximization problem (Equation~\eqref{eq:theory:mca_maximization}) for these augmented data, capturing the temporal evolution of coupled patterns. So far, it seems that Hilbert MCA has seen limited application in climate science \cite[e.g.][]{elipot_observed_2017}. 


\subsection{From Covariance to Correlation: The CPCCA Framework}\label{sec:theory:cpcca}
While the MCA framework effectively identifies shared variability between two datasets by maximizing squared covariance, it does not necessarily optimize correlation. The distinction between covariance and correlation becomes particularly significant when the datasets operate on vastly different scales. Moreover, there are scenarios where maximizing correlation is more important than achieving high covariance.

Canonical Correlation Analysis (CCA) \cite{hotelling_relations_1936} addresses this by identifying shared patterns that maximize correlation:

\begin{align}\label{eq:theory:cca_maximization}
    \argmax_{\bm{v}_{x,1}, \bm{v}_{y,1}} \, \bm{v}_{x,1}^H \bm{C}_{xy} \bm{v}_{y,1} \quad \text{subject to} \quad \Vab{\bm{v}_{x,1}^H \bm{Z}_x}_2 = \Vab{\bm{v}_{y,1}^H \bm{Z}_y}_2 = 1,
\end{align}

Unlike MCA, where pattern vectors $\bm{V}_{s}$ are orthogonal, resulting in correlated expansion coefficients, CCA produces pattern vectors that are not necessarily orthogonal but yield uncorrelated expansion coefficients, known as canonical variates.

However, a common drawback of CCA is that its leading modes may exhibit strong correlations while accounting for only a small portion of the overall variance in each dataset. To mitigate this, CCA is often performed in a reduced PC space, focusing on the most significant modes of variability and reducing computational demands \cite[][pp. 320-321]{storch_statistical_1999}. Despite these advantages, this approach can overlook important correlations involving lower-variance PCs, as noted by \citet[][p. 223]{jolliffe_principal_2002}.

In practice, a balance is desired between maximizing covariance (as in MCA) and maximizing correlation (as in CCA). To achieve this, \citet{swenson_continuum_2015} introduced the Continuum Power Canonical Correlation Analysis (CPCCA) framework \cite{swenson_continuum_2015}. CPCCA partially whitens\footnote{Whitening, also known as sphering, refers to the process of transforming a dataset so that its features or variables are uncorrelated and each has unit variance. This often involves adjusting the data to have a zero mean and an identity covariance matrix. See \citet{delsole_predictability_2007} for more details on its relevance in climate science.} the datasets using a fractional whitening parameter, $\alpha_s$, allowing for a continuous adjustment between maximizing correlation (CCA) and maximizing covariance (MCA). This provides a flexible framework to identify shared patterns between datasets.

In this thesis, we extended the CPCCA framework similarly to our approach with MCA by applying it to Hilbert complexified data and incorporating an additional rotation step for enhanced interpretability, resulting in rotated Hilbert CPCCA. In Chapter~\ref{chapter:tele}, we utilized both MCA and CPCCA to identify and interpret ocean-atmospheric teleconnections in the climate system.
