\chapter{Shared Dynamics between Temporally Lagged Climate Variables: A Case Study of SST and Continental Precipitation}

Weather conditions in one region can affect those in another, distant location through what is commonly called an atmospheric “teleconnection.” For example, the El Niño Southern Oscillation (ENSO) occurring in the Pacific can influence conditions in the Atlantic through its effect on the extratropical jet stream. \citet{scaife_enso_2024} showed that ENSO can have a delayed effect as well: The Arctic Oscillation and the North Atlantic Oscillation have an opposite response delayed by 1 year. The authors explain this behavior as being the result of migrating atmospheric angular momentum anomalies.

\section{Methods \& Data}

We use ERA5 monthly averaged reanalysis \cite{c3s_era5_monthly_single} for sea surface temperature (SST) and continental precipitation to investigate the shared dynamics between these two variables on seasonal to decadal time scales. The ERA5 dataset provides global coverage on a regular longitude-latitude grid with a spatial resolution of \ang{0.25} and a monthly temporal resolution.  The time frame of the dataset spans from 1940 to today (given a couple of days delay), allowing us to capture long-term climate variability and trends and potential teleconnnections between both variables.

\paragraph{Data Preprocessing} We focus on continental precipitation by masking out precipitation over the ocean using the land-ocean mask at 1:110m scale provided by Natural Earth\footnote{\url{naturalearthdata.com}}. Temporally, we want to focus on teleconnection playing out on annual to decadal time scale, this is why we a apply a centered 7 month centered moving average to both the SST and precipitation time series. This way, we remove the high frequency variability such as monsoonal cirulcations but keep the low frequency variability such as ENSO, PDO, etc. We do not remove the seasonal cycle, however , in order to test if we can recover dominant modes of variability with rotated Hilbert MCA (Section~\ref{sec:theory:mca}) despite having the predominant seasonal cycle in the data. In this case study, we want to focus on teleconnections, i.e. connections between SST and continental precipitation in regions that are not necessarily close to each other. In contrast,  local connections between SST and precipipitation are quite common, for instance, coastal precipitation and river plumes are related to changes in SST \todo{look for reference}. Since the high-spatial resolution of ERA5 (ca. 30 km at the equator) is able to resolve these processes, it is likely that a MCA of both datasets will also identify these processes. To mitigate this, we apply spatial averages to coarsen the data from a \ang{0.25} to a \ang{1.00} longitude--latitude grid so that the impact of small-scale processes is hopefully smoothed out. This processing step leaves us with \num{36588} and \num{16920} non-masked grid cells (or features) for SST and continental precipitation. To counter the effect of grid size distortion towards the poles \cite{north_latitude_1982}, we apply a latitude weighting to the data as described in Section~\ref{sec:theory:pca}.

We then apply the Hilbert transform to each time series individually in both datasets to complexfiy both datasets. To counter potentially boundary effects in the time series introduced by the Hilbert transform due to spectral leakage (Section~\ref{sec:theory:hilbert_pca} \todo{still to be added}), we extend the time series of both SST and continental precipitation in both directions by an expoentially decaying function with an e-folding time of 3 months so that the final time series has the length three times for the original ones. The value of 3 months is motivated by the fact that the expected dominant time scales in the analyzsed data is the seasonal cycle with a period of 12 months, so that after 6 months any periodical signal will have decayed to a negligible value.

Since the computation of the cross-covariance matrix can get very large we perform Hilbert MCA in the principal component space, i.e. we transform each data set into the space spanned by its principal axes and considering for each data sets as many PCs that explain in total \qty{99}{\percent} of each data set, which leaves us with 74 and 199 components for SST and continental precipitation, so that the cross-covariance in the PC reduced space has a dimensions of 74 x 199. By reducing the dimensionality we also render the initially ill-conditioned problem (about $10^5$ features vs only about $10^3$ samples) into a well-conditioned problem, produdcing a more regularized solution.


We then perform MCA on the complexified, PC-transformed data to identify the shared patterns of variability between SST and continental precipitation. 

As discussed in Chapter 2, there are two aspects of the standard MCA solution that can be undesireable. First the obtained spatial are dense, i.e. almost all grid points contribute to a given pattern which can make them hard to interpert. Second, the obtained expansion coefficients are uncorrelated with each other. Although appealing from a mathematical perspective, when interpreting the obtained patterns and time series, this can be an undesireable feature, since there's no obvious reason why geophysical processes should be uncorrelated in general. 

To address these two points we apply an additional rotation as discussed by \citet{cheng_orthogonal_1995} (see Chapter 2).

There are two questions that must be addressed before rotation: 1) Orthogonal or oblique rotation? 2) How many modes to rotate?

\paragraph{How many modes to rotate?} The answer here lies to find a good balanace between two counteracting ideas. On the one hand side, we want to rotate as little modes as possible so that we avoid including lots of the noise presumbly present in higher modes.  But on the other hand side, we want to include at least as many modes that we include most of the important shared variability. The final result should also be stable in terms of number of modes to be rotated, so we want to included as many modes, that some modes more ore less won't make a big change in terms of rotated solution, because the included modes just carry so little overall information that it doesn't really change anything. So that's the balance we want to strike. 

Now, in MCA--for which the rotation was first applied to by \citet{cheng_orthogonal_1995}--each mode is typically measred in terms of squared covariance, and the sqaured covariance fraction (SCF) (Equation~\ref{eq:theory:mca:scf}) provides a natural and simple measure to evaliate the modes' importance. Since we moved our problem into the PC space, the total shared variability can be summed up by in total 74 modes. Evaluting the SCF for the first non-rotated modes (Figure~\ref{fig:tele:singular_spectrum}~A) shows that virtually all the squared covariance is captured within the first six modes after which the individual SCF drops below \qty{0.01}{\percent}. Note however, that compared to PCA where we evaluate the modes in terms of explained variance, the squared nature of the SCF will naturally lead to a much faster decay of the SCF. 

In the end, we want to have a solution that is largely insensitive to the number of rotated modes. Consquently, we choose to apply an objective criterion to determine the number of modes to rotate where the solution is the most stable. For this, we iteratively rotate the first $k$ modes, $k \in [10, 40 ]$, which results in $31$ unique solutions. We then compute for each solution the amplitude patterns of the complex-valued singular vectors of the first 10 modes for both SST and precipitation. We argue that if the solution is stable, the amplitude patterns shouldn't drastically change from one rotated solution to another. However, given the small fraction of explained squared covariance for higher modes (Figure~\ref{fig:tele:singular_spectrum}~A) we expect that the order of modes may change slightly. Further, we assume that stable and physically interesting modes will be within the first 10 modes. 

To evaluate the stability of the 31 unique solutions we compute the similarity between the amplitude patterns of the first 10 modes using the cosine similarity\footnote{also known as Tuckers coefficient of congruence}--a non-centered version of the Pearson correlation coefficient--between each possible pair of the 10 amplitude patterns. The cosine similarity ranges from -1 to 1, where 1 indicates that the two patterns are identical, 0 indicates that the two patterns are orthogonal to each other, and -1 indicates that the two patterns are anti-correlated. So for each one of the 31 unique solutions, we compare it to the other 30 solutions. This is done by doing the following: For each unique solution, we compare the first 10 amplitude patterns to the first 10 amplitude patterns of the other 30 solutions, by computing the cosine similarity between each possible pair of patterns. We then look if for a given solution and a given mode, the same mode can be found in any other of the first 10 modes of the other solutions. We consider two patterns two be the same if the cosine similarity is above 0.9. Then for each solution we compute the mode stability index $\chi$ as the fraction of modes that are the same in any of the other solutions. The mode stability index $\chi$ is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:tele:singular_spectrum}~D. We find that for both SST and precipitation patterns the highest stability is obtained when 22 modes are rotated, which more rotated modes not changing the solution significantly. In the following we will restrict our discussion to the solution obtained by rotating the first 22 modes.


\paragraph{Orthogonal or oblique rotation?} The choice between orthogonal and oblique rotation is a trade-off between interpretability and complexity. Oblique  rotations can produce much more "simple structure" \cite{richman_rotation_1986}, i.e. spatial patterns that are restricted to very few grid points, and thus very localized modes which are typically straightforward to interpret. This benefit of simplified spatial interpretability however comes at a cost of allowing the expansions coefficents (time series) to be correlated, thus allowing for some "mode mixing" within the expansion coefficients.Orthogonal rotation ensures that the obtained expansion coefficients are uncorrelated with each other, thus creating clearly dinstinguishable time series. However, in the context of geophysical data, it is not clear why the expansion coefficients should be uncorrelated. In fact, it is more likely that the expansion coefficients are correlated with each other, since geophysical processes are often linked to each other. Therefore, we choose to start with an oblique rotation to the MCA solution and only use orthogonal varimax rotation in case that the obtained mode are indeed only very weakly correlated, i.e. exhibting absolute pairwise correlation coefficients of the obtained expansion coefficients below \num{0.2}. For an initial rotation to check the degree of correlation between the expansion coefficients, we choose to rotate the first 22 modes with the promax rotation scheme \cite{hendrickson_promax_1964} with a weak rotation power of $p=2$, as recommended by \citet{richman_rotation_1986}. Computing the absolute pairwise correlation coefficients between the first 10 promax-rotated, complex-valued expansion coefficients results in in Pearson corrleation coefficients below \num{0.14} suggesting indeed only weak correlation between the leading modes. We therefore choose to restrict the following discussion to the orthogonal rotation case (promax rotation power $p=1$). However, we present the results of the oblique rotation in the appendix (Figure~\ref{app:fig:tele:mode01_a100_p2}--\ref{app:fig:tele:mode10_a100_p2}). 



\paragraph{Climate Indices of known Climate Oscillations}

Climate indices of monthly means, used in this study for thesake of comparison, are downloaded from the websites of the
National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA; https://psl.noaa.gov/data/climateindices/list/), namely the oceanic Niño index (ONI) provided by the NOAA Climate Prediction Center, the Pacific decadal oscillation (PDO; Mantua and Hare 2002), and the Atlantic meridional mode (AMM; Chiang and Vimont 2004). We calculate the ENSO
Modoki index (EMI; Ashok et al. 2007) according to $EMI = R_A - \frac{1}{2}R_B - \frac{1}{2}R_C$, where $R$ denotes the area-averaged ERA5 SST anomalies over the regions $A$ (\ang{165}E-\ang{140}W; \ang{10}S-\ang{10}N), $B$ (\ang{110}-\ang{70}W; \ang{15}S-\ang{5}N), and $C$ (\ang{125}E-\ang{145}E; \ang{10}S-\ang{20}N), respectively. Furthermore, we define an oceanic warming index (OWI) as the area average of ERA5 SST anomalies (\ang{60}S-\ang{70}N) using the same latitude area weighting as for the MCA data preparation. Finally, all indices are smoothed using a centered 7-month moving average in alignment with our data preprocessing. All climate indices are restricted to be within 1940 to 2022. We then detrend all indices, except the OWI, using linear regression. We further center and standardize all indices for better comparison with our obtained expansion coefficients.

\paragraph{Power Spectral Density}
We compute power spectral density (PSD) for the first 10 expansion coefficients of SST and continental precipitation, as well as for the climate indices. For the complex-valued expansion coefficients, we take the real part after having phase shifted the the expansion coefficients such that for each mode the phase shift at the location with maximum absolute value of the hetereogeneous patterns of SST is zero. This step is necessary because the complex-valued singular vectors from our Hilbert MCA are only defined up to a phase shift. This step ensures that the real part represents the maximum fraction of the lagged variability and thus represents the time evolution of the strongest signal in the data. Addtiionally, prior to PSD computation, all time series are linearly detrended. The PSD is computed using Welch's Overlapped Segment Averaging (WOSA) method \cite[e.g.][chapter 8.8]{percival_spectral_2020} with \num{256} data points per segment and a Hanning window with \qty{50}{\percent} overlap resulting in a total of six segments. All PSDs are normalized to represent fraction of energy density per frequency band.

Approximate confidence intervals of PSDs are often computed using the $\chi^2$-distribution, however, this is only valid for a particular frequency and is not directly applicable for a set of frequencies or over the entire frequency range \cite[cf.][p. 468]{priestley_spectral_1981}. Since we don't know \textit{a priori} which frequencies are of interest, we use the circular moving block bootstrap \cite{politis_circular_1991} to estimate confidence intervals of the PSDs.  We generate \num{10000} bootstrap samples by resampling the original time series with replacement and a block length of \qty{9}{years}. The choice of 9 years is a compromise between having enough independent bootstrapping samples and long enough blocks to resolve the frequencies of interest. Note that due to this choice, we cannot resolve periodiciies longer than 9 years because we effectiviely scramble the autocorrelation function beyond this time lag. Additionally, we only consider the months of January as valid block starting points so that we keep intact the annual cycle. We then compute the PSD for each of the bootstrap samples. The \num{2.5} and \num{97.5} percentiles of the bootstrap PSDs are then used as the lower and upper bounds of the confidence intervals.

Since climate data is often characterized by a `red noise' spectrum, we compare the PSDs of the expansion coefficients against a theoretical `red noise' spectrum \cite[e.g.][chapter 10]{wilks_statistical_2019}. We estimate the `red noise' spectrum using an first-order autoregressive process, AR(1) process, and compute the theoretical PSD using the formula given by \citet{gilman_power_1963}. We estimate the autoregressive coefficient $\rho$ from the data as the lag-1 autocorrelation coefficient. The null hypothesis is then tested by comparing the spectral density of the data to the spectral density of the AR(1) process using the $F$-statistic using a significance level of $\alpha=0.01$.

\paragraph{Mode 1 -- The Seasonal Cycle} Describing more than \qty{99}{\percent} of the squared covariance between SST and continental precipitation ($\text{SCF}_1=\qty{99.1}{\percent}$) with a clear one-year periodicity, mode 1 clearly describes the annual cycle (Figure \ref{fig:tele:mode01_a100}, \ref{fig:tele:psd}~A). The mode exhibits also a very strong link between SST and continental precipitation as evidenceed by the Pearson correlation coefficient obetween the two expansion coefficients of $r_{ec,1}=0.99$. As expected, the annual cycle shows itself in both variables on a global scale, with strong expression around the extra-tropics for SST and within tropics for precipitation. Expected exceptions from this are, for instance, transition zones, like north of the Arabian Sea where precipitation switches from e.g. summer to winter-dominated precipitation regime and both regimes are equally important in a very narrow stretch, or other regions that are characterized by more than one rainy seasons, like e.g. central east Africa.

The phase functions (Figure~\ref{fig:tele:mode01_a100}~C, D) correctly separates the seasonal cycle mode according to the timing of annual peak, e.g. identifying the anticorrelation (phase shift of about $\pi$) between the northern and southern oceans. For the SST we also notice that the eastern equatorial Pacific and the equatorial Indian Ocean show a positive phase shift of $+\pi/4$ (or about six weeks) relative to the dominant seasonal cycle in the ocean. On the continents, the phase function illustrates the division into January-February (light blue) and July-August (dark-red) dominated rainfall systems and identifies corresponding transition zones, as for example over the South American rain forest and central North America. It also highlights some interesting dynamical regions that stand out from their respective environments, namely, the west and east coasts of North America, the Mediterranean region, and, to a lesser degree, the East Asian monsoon region in China. 

We also note that in general this first mode explains much more of the variance in SST (\qty{80.4}{\percent}) than it does in continental precipitation (\qty{62.1}{\percent}). This makes sense as the seasonal cycle is a much more dominant feature in SST than it is in continental precipitation with the latter being generally a much more noisy variable with a much more complex spatio-temporal structure. Specifically, continental precipitation is much more driven directly by atmospheric variability which has a very high internal variability. Also, the ocean's thermal inertia allows to absorb or release large amounts of heat with only minor changes in temperature, as a results SSTs are in general less variable than atmopsheric variables. And finally, while SSTs change smoothly and slowly over vast spatial scales, continental precipitation is a very localized variable, such that interannual variations in global circulation patterns can produce drastic changes in local precipitation patterns.

\paragraph{Mode 2 -- ENSO} Mode 2 ($\text{SCF}_2=\qty{3.9}{\percent}, \, r_{ec,2}=0.91$) can be clearly associated with the ocean-atmosphere phenomenon of ENSO (Figure~\ref{fig:tele:mode02_a100}) with the Pearson correlation coefficient between the expansion coefficents of SST with the ONI revealing a strong correlation of $r_{2, ONI}=0.92$ indicating the strong association between this mode and ENSO. Secondly, many of the resulting teleconnections shown in Figure~\ref{fig:tele:mode02_a100}~A-D have been reported and analyzed before. For instance, during El Niño, higher SST in the central and eastern Pacific and lower SST in the western Pacific positively correlate with heavier rainfall within a narrowband along the west coast and the southeastern coast of South America \cite{tedeschi_influences_2013}, the east and west coasts of North America \cite{ropelewski_north_1986}, the East Asian monsoon region \cite{wen_direct_2019}, and the Horn of Africa \cite{indeje_enso_2000}. At the same time, precipitation decreases in northern South America \cite{tedeschi_influences_2013}, Oceania \cite{dai_global_2000}, South Africa \cite{gaughan_inter-_2016}, and the Indian monsoon region \cite{cherchi_influence_2013}. During La Niña (the counter-phase of El Niño), these correlations are reversed. Recently, similar teleconnections have been identified via event coincidence analysis \cite{wiedermann_differential_2021}. For a relatively recent summary of established ENSO-related rainfall patterns during El Niño and La Niña see \cite{lenssen_seasonal_2020}. 

Additional, our result show the lagged dynamics between the core region in the western and central Pacific and the Indian Ocean \cite{krishnamurthy_variability_2003}, the South China Sea \cite{klein_remote_1999}, and the tropical North Atlantic \cite{enfield_tropical_1997,saravanan_interaction_2000, alexander_influence_2002, chiang_tropical_2002} in accordance with previous studies. For instance, it was found that the ENSO-related SST teleconnections in the remote oceans often occur with some delays, with the Indian Ocean typically peaking 3 months and the South China Sea and tropical North Atlantic 5 months after the SST peak in the Pacific ENSO region \cite{enfield_tropical_1997,klein_remote_1999,saravanan_interaction_2000}. The mechanism behind these lagged responses, known as an atmospheric bridge, is based on the characteristic atmospheric circulation during El Niño, which causes changes in cloud cover and evaporation over the remote oceans, leading to increased net heat flux and SSTs \cite{lau_role_1996,klein_remote_1999}. However, due to the broadband frequency spectrum of the SST PC (Figure~\ref{fig:tele:psd}~B), with most of the energy contained betweem a periodicity of roughly 2.5 and 4 years, the phase cannot exactly be translated into a time shift. Assuming these two peridicity limits we can however compute an approximate time shift, for instance for the tropical Atlantic which exhibits a phase shift of $+\pi/2$, which would then translates into time shifts of between 7.5 to 13.5 months, which is close to but slightly larger than the observed average time lag of 5 months.  Nevertheless, the tropical North Atlantic clearly exhibits more positive phase values compared to the Pacific El Niño region, therefore indicating to be positively time shifted relative to the Pacific.

Recently, \citet{scaife_enso_2024} made news by providing evidence for an even longer time lag, a 1-year lagged extratropical response to the ENSO, which they found to be strongest in the North Atlantic. While they used sea surface pressure as an observational variable to asses the ENSO teleconnection, our analysis based on SST also indicates a weak signal of shared variability with ENSO in the North Atlantic, being phase shifted by about $+3\pi/4$. Using our rough limits of periodicity established from our analysis, this lag would translate in time shifts between 11.25 and 18 months, which is very much in line with the declared 1-year lagged response by \cite{scaife_enso_2024}.

Taking all the lagged responses together, ENSO explains about \qty{5.8}{\percent} in SST variability and about \qty{4.6}{\percent} in continental precipitation since 1940.

\paragraph{Mode 3 -- Global Warming} 
Mode 3 ($\text{SCF}_3=\qty{2.0}{\percent}, \, r_{ec,3}=0.80$) represents the changes in SST and the associated changes in precipitation patterns (Figure~\ref{fig:tele:mode03_a100}) due to global warming as evidence by the strong correlation with the oceanic warming index (OWI), $r_{OWI}=0.78$. The warming SST patterns clearly emerge in all major ocean basins, with a clear spatial focus along the equator except the eastern equatorial Pacific (Figure~\ref{fig:tele:mode03_a100}~A). Additionally, the amplitudes are slightly more pronounced in the Northern Hemisphere due to the asymmetric response of the northern and southern trade winds to global warming \cite{xie_global_2010}. We also note a pronounced warming of the western part of both the Pacific and the Atlantic basins, both regions of enhanced ocean heat transport \cite{xie_global_2010,hannachi_archetypal_2017}. 

Similar to these oceanic trends, we also observe global trends in the precipitation patterns, with the highest amplitudes in central Africa and parts of Oceania, South and North America as well as central Asia (Figure~\ref{fig:tele:mode03_a100}~B). From the phase functions we can deduce an increase in precipitation (phase shift of zero) around the tropical Oceania, while a decrease in precipitation (phase shift of $\pm \pi$) is suggested for central Africa, central South Africa, western North America as well as the Arabian peninsula and surrounding regions. These results are in strong spatial agreement with trends obtained from reanalysis data for both SST and continental precipitation (Figure~\ref{fig:tele:trends}), and agree also largely with studies based on observational data \cite{gu_spatial_2015} and, more recently, on CMIP5 climate simulations \cite{giorgi_response_2019}. All these results support the idea of this mode representing the shared variability due to global warming.

Some minor descrepeancies exists though between the obtained phase functions and the computed trends from reanalysis data. Notably, reanalysis data indicates strong positive trends of about \qty{1}{mm/decade} in the eastern part of North America, which is not evident in the phase function. Also, the phase functions indicate an increase in precipitatoin in the southern parts of the Indian subcontinent. The trends exhibited by reanalysis data clearly show a more complicated picture, with an increase in the southeast and a decrease in the western part.

Finally, it should be stressed that expansion coefficients for both SST and precipitation, only provide a meaningful interpretation for phases of zero (correlation) and $\pm \pi$ (anticorrelation), due to their broadband power frequency spectra (Figure~\ref{fig:tele:psd}~C). For phase shifts different from that, no clear conclusions can be drawn, as for example is the case for western Australia where the phase shift is approximately $-\pi/4$.

\paragraph{Mode 4 - Nonlinear ENSO modulation} Mode 4 ($\text{SCF}_4=\qty{0.5}{\percent}, \, r_{ec,4}=0.82$) describes an oscillating SST anomaly mainly limited to the central Pacific (Figure~\ref{fig:tele:mode04_a100}) describing a pattern that was popularzized by \citet{ashok_nino_2007} which they termed `El Niño Modoki' and can be represented by the EMI ($r_{EMI}=0.56$). This pattern consists of higher SST in the central Pacific and lower SST in the eastern Pacific which correlate with reduced precipitation in the East Asian monsoon region \cite{feng_different_2011}, Australia \cite{taschetto_nino_2009}, parts of South America \cite{tedeschi_influences_2013}, and South Africa \cite{ratnam_remote_2014} and vice versa. Some of these teleconnections have recently been revealed by event coincidence analysis \cite{wiedermann_differential_2021} although our analysis suggests additional important links to the East Asian monsoon region.  Later research by \citet{takahashi_enso_2011}, however, indicated that El Niño Modoki and the classical type are in fact one but that the perceived divergent behavior highlights the dynamic range of the system, specifically the non-linearity of ENSO. Following this interpretation, the obtained teleconnections in our analysis should not be interpreted as a separate mode but rather as a modulation of ENSO mode 2 by the non-linear behavior of the system. 

The power spectral density of this mode shows a significant peak at around \num{1.5} years (Figure~\ref{fig:tele:psd}~D), but lacks the energy at periodicites tyipically associated with ENSO, namely 2 to 7 years. In general, the mode remains however rather broadband which prevents a clear phase interpreation apart from  zero and $\pm \pi$ phase shifts.

\paragraph{Mode 6 -- AMM} Mode 6 ($\text{SCF}_6=\qty{0.2}{\percent}, \, r_{ec,6}=0.85$) is characterized by an SST pattern concentrated in the tropical and subarctic North Atlantic (Figure~\ref{fig:tele:mode06_a100}~A) that explains about \qty{0.7}{\percent} of the overall SST variability. The SST pattern, representing the Atlantic meridional mode (AMM) ($r_{AMM}=0.75$), is the dominant coupled ocean-atmospheric phenomenon in the tropical Atlantic \cite{servain_relationship_1999} and its impacts on precipitation -- explaining about \qty{0.8}{\percent} of total precipitation variability --  of the African Sahel zone, Central America, and northern South American continent are well known \cite{lamb_interannual_1986,martin_multidecadal_2014}. Only recently, \citet{vittal_early_2020} found a link between the AMM and Indian summer monsoon rainfall, which they used to improve precipitation forecast. In addition, our result suggests more teleconnections between the AMM and precipitation variability over North America, the Mediterranean, East Africa, and the East Asian monsoon region, providing much potential for advancing local rainfall predictions in those areas. Of particular interest could be the region of the southeastern North America, where a phase shift of $-3\pi/4$ -- corresponding to a time lag of about 9 months assuming a periodicity of 2 years (Figure~\ref{fig:tele:psd}~E) -- is observed.

\paragraph{Mode 9 -- PDO} Mode 9 ($\text{SCF}_9=\qty{0.2}{\percent}, \, r_{ec,9}=0.78$) shows a slow-oscillating pattern of SST in the northeastern Pacific correlating with localized precipitation
patterns distributed over all continents, preodimantly in North America (Figure~\ref{fig:tele:mode09_a100}~A,B). The typical spatial SST pattern is known as the Pacific decadal oscillation (PDO) \cite{mantua_pacific_1997} and a well-established climate index. A combination of various processes originating in the tropics and extratropics has been proposed as the physical source of the PDO \cite{newman_pacific_2016}, with ENSO and PDO likely responding to the same forcing function \cite{pierce_role_2002}. Our analysis, however, provides a means to disentangle ENSO- and PDO-related precipitation patterns, which are often similar for western North America \cite{hu_interferential_2009}. Typically, the PDO exhibit strong energy around periodicities of 30 years, yet due to our preprocessing which used block sizes of 9 years we cannot resolve such long periodicities in our analysis. We find however that higher-frequency, multi-year periodiciets of around 4 years (Figure~\ref{fig:tele:psd}~I), which is likely because of the important feedbacks between the ENSO and PDO mode \cite{mills_seasonal_2013, tang_positive_2024}. This finding is also interesting because this spectral peak is not visible in the official PDO index and indicates slight differences in the composition of the PDO mode in our analysis compared to the traditional PDO index.

\paragraph{Mode 10 - IOD} Mode 10 ($\text{SCF}_10=\qty{0.2}{\percent}, \, r_{ec,10}=0.83$) resembles the Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD) ($r_{IOD}=0.49$), a coupled ocean-atmosphere phenomenon in the tropical Indian Ocean (Saji et al. 1999) that explains about \qty{0.4}{\percent} of the total SST variability. The SST pattern is characterized by a dipole structure with higher SST in the western and lower SST in the eastern Indian Ocean. The associated precipitation pattern is mainly concentrated in the surrounding tropical land masses, namely central east Africa and Oceania, explaining \qty{1.2}{\percent} of the total precipitation variability (Figure~\ref{fig:tele:mode10_a100}~A,B). The phase function shows a clear phase shift of $+\pi/2$, i.e. anti-correlation between prcipitation patterns around the eastern and western Indian ocean, mirroring the dipole in the tropical indian SSTs. Note that our results has only a weakly expressed dipole, with a much stronger center of attention in the eastern Indian ocean and much lower amplitude in the western Indian ocean (Figure~\ref{fig:tele:mode10_a100}~C,D).  Although the power spectral density of this mode shows significant energy contained around 18-24 months compared to the red-noise model, the spectral density is relatively flat for a wide range of frequencies, which shows the rather aperiodic and irregular nature of the IOD (Figure~\ref{fig:tele:psd}~J).



\paragraph{Other modes of shared variability}
In our discussion above we have focused mostly on the modes of shared variability identified in \citet{rieger_lagged_2021}. We note, however, that here in this varimax-rotated Hilbert MCA of the spatially and temporally extended datasets, we find some additional, apparently important modes of variability that we have omitted so far from the discussion, namely modes 5, 7 and 8. 
For instance, Mode 5 (Figure~\ref{aapp:fig:tele:mode05_a100_p1}) looks very similar to parts of the atmospheric Pacific-South American (PSA) mode which acts to excite multiscale spatiotemporal responses in the upper South Pacific Ocean and which was found to be the key driver of oceanic variability in the South Pacific subtropics that generates a potentially predictable climate signal linked to the tropics \cite{lou_linking_2021}. As part of the associated rainfall patterns \citet{mo_pacificsouth_2001} found enhanced rainfall over southeastern South America similar to rainfall anomalies during ENSO, which we also identify as part of the teleconnections in mode 5 (Figure~\ref{app:fig:tele:mode05_a100_p1}~B,D). However, the observed rainfall deficits over northeastern Brazil is not evident in our mode.
Additoinally, mode 7 (Figure~\ref{app:fig:tele:mode07_a100_p1}) shows another pattern of variability focus in the east Indian ocean and precipitation teleconnections focus on central Africa and the southeastern North American continent. Finally, mode 8 (Figure~\ref{app:fig:tele:mode08_a100_p1}) shows a SST pattern of variability that is dominated by regions north of the Arctic circle showing a positive trend superposed by a slow frequency variability which may be another mode linked to global warming but with a different spatial focus and expression, possibly related to arctic amplification.

\paragraph{An what if we maximize correlation? Applying the CPCCA framework}

An important factor in the CPCCA is the degree of fractional whitening $\alpha_i$ \cite{swenson_continuum_2015} where the index $i$ refers to either of the two datasets, SST or continental precipitation (PRCP), $i \in \{\text{SST, PRCP}\}$. Since the two datasets live on different scales, maximizing correlation rather than covariance is of interest. In the CPCCA framework which bridges maximization of correlation and covariance on a continuous scale via the adjustable parameters $\alpha_i$, this can be achieved by choosing small values of $\alpha_i$. Here we choose $\alpha_i = 0.2$ for both datasets following the results of \citet{swenson_continuum_2015} that showed that CPCCA tended to be the most skillfull in terms of capturing the shared variability between two synthetic climate datasets for $\alpha_i$ around \numrange{0.15}{0.20}.

The recent Python libary cca-zoo provides a plethora of non-linear, kernel or neural network based CCA methods (cca-zoo) which can greatly facilitate future analysis.

In any case, so far kernel methods for dimensionality reduction have only found limited application in climate science (bueso, hannachi). One reason may be that understanding the underlying spatial pattern is often a core desire in climate science, which is not straightforward with kernel methods, as the spatial patterns are often computed implicitly in a high-dimensional feature space giving rise to the so-called pre-image problem. To circument this issue, a linear projection of the patterns was proposed as an approximation (bueso) or computing composite patterns based on the probabilits distribution of the kernel PCs (hannachi).
