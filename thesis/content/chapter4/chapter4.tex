\chapter{Gaussian Process Modeling}\label{chapter:gpr}

Environmental data often reside at opposite ends of a complexity spectrum. On one side, we may have dense, high-dimensional observations (e.g., satellite imagery or climate reanalyses) sampled regularly in space and time. On the other side, datasets may consist of only a few measurements, sampled irregularly over large areas or long periods. In this chapter, we focus on the latter scenario---specifically, spatially sparse and irregularly sampled datasets. We introduce Gaussian processes (GPs) as a flexible, nonparametric framework for identifying patterns and making predictions in these settings. By placing a prior distribution over an infinite-dimensional space of functions, GPs adapt naturally to the complexity of the observed data and provide principled uncertainty quantification.

Gaussian process models have been widely applied in regression, classification, and spatial statistics. They encompass a variety of well-known techniques, unifying them under a single, coherent framework. For instance, Bayesian linear regression arises from a GP with a linear kernel \cite[cf.][]{mackay_introduction_1998}, kriging in geostatistics can be viewed as GP interpolation with a specific class of covariance functions \cite[cf.][]{cressie_statistics_1993}, and the Kalman filter---ubiquitous in weather forecasting and data assimilation \cite[e.g.][]{evensen_ensemble_2003}---can be interpreted as a special case of GP regression in the state-space domain\footnote{This equivalence holds under the assumption of a linear-Gaussian state-space model with a GP kernel that exactly mirrors the covariance structure of that model; see, e.g., \citet{hartikainen_kalman_2010} for a detailed discussion.}.

This chapter provides a concise introduction to Gaussian processes and their relevant extensions for this thesis. We first outline the basic framework and theory of GPs (Section~\ref{sec:gp:intro}) and its Bayesian embedding (Section~\ref{sec:gp:bayes}), followed by their application in regression tasks (Section~\ref{sec:gp:regression}). We then extend the discussion to generalized Gaussian process models (GGPMs) for non-Gaussian likelihoods (Section~\ref{sec:gp:generalized}), and finally explore the application of GPs in spatial and spatio-temporal modeling (Section~\ref{sec:gp:spatial}). These foundations set the stage for the log-Gaussian Cox process framework employed in Chapter~\ref{chapter:beach_litter} to analyze sparse observations of macroplastic pollution along European coastlines.

Sections~\ref{sec:gp:intro}~to~\ref{sec:gp:regression} follow standard references such as \citet{rasmussen_gaussian_2006}, \citet{bishop_pattern_2006}, and \citet{mackay_introduction_1998}, which provide a deeper exploration of Gaussian process theory and methods.

\section{Introduction to Gaussian Processes}\label{sec:gp:intro}

A Gaussian process is a nonparametric\footnote{Although GPs are often described as "nonparametric", they do have hyperparameters (e.g., length-scales, variances). The term "nonparametric" here refers to the infinite-dimensional function space being modeled, rather than the absence of any parameters. In other words, the GP prior does not restrict the function to a specific finite-parameter family (like polynomials of fixed degree) but instead allows the data to determine the function's complexity, subject to the kernel assumptions.} Bayesian model that places a prior distribution over functions, rather than over a finite set of parameters. In contrast to parametric models with fixed complexity, GPs allow complexity to arise naturally from the data. They assume that any finite collection of function values follows a joint Gaussian distribution, enabling flexible modeling of complex, nonlinear relationships. This is particularly appealing in environmental modeling, where the underlying physical processes are often intricate and not easily reduced to a small set of parameters.

A GP $f(\bm{x})$ is fully characterized by its mean function $m(\bm{x})$ and covariance function $k(\bm{x}, \bm{x}')$. Formally, we write:

\begin{equation}
    f(\bm{x}) \sim \GP \ab(m(\bm{x}), k(\bm{x}, \bm{x}')).
\end{equation}

For any finite set of $N$ points $\{\bm{x}_i\}_{i=1}^N$, $\bm{x}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{D}$, the random vector

\[
\bm{f} = \begin{pmatrix} f(\bm{x}_1) \\ \vdots \\ f(\bm{x}_N) \end{pmatrix}
\]

follows a multivariate Gaussian distribution:

\begin{equation}
    \bm{f} \sim \mathcal{N}\ab(\bm{m},\, \bm{K}),
\end{equation}

where $\bm{m} = \ab( m(\bm{x}_1),\dots,m(\bm{x}_N) )^T$ and $\bm{K}$ is the $N\times N$ covariance matrix with entries $K_{ij} = k(\bm{x}_i, \bm{x}_j)$.

In practice, the mean function is often set to zero without loss of generality because the data can be centered or a constant mean term can be learned separately. The real modeling power stems from the covariance function, or \emph{kernel},

\begin{equation}
    k(\bm{x},\bm{x}')=\mathbb{E}\ab[(f(\bm{x})-m(\bm{x}))(f(\bm{x}')-m(\bm{x}'))],    
\end{equation}

which encodes assumptions about function smoothness, variability and correlations across the input domain.

A valid kernel must be positive semidefinite, ensuring that for any finite set of inputs the resulting covariance matrix is non-negative definite. This requirement is closely connected to Mercer's theorem, which states that any continuous, symmetric, positive semidefinite kernel can be expressed in terms of an infinite sum of eigenfunctions \cite[][p.96]{rasmussen_gaussian_2006}. In addition, Bochner's theorem characterizes stationary kernels as the Fourier transform of a non-negative measure, linking the spatial domain properties to the spectral domain \cite[][p.82]{rasmussen_gaussian_2006}.

Many standard kernels incorporate structural assumptions that enforce these properties. A common choice is the squared exponential (or Radial Basis Function, RBF) kernel:

\begin{align}
    k(\bm{x},\bm{x}') = \eta^2 \exp \ab(-\frac{\ab \| \bm{x}-\bm{x}' \|^2}{2\ell^2}),
\end{align}

where $\eta^2$ sets the overall variance and $\ell$ is the characteristic length-scale controlling how quickly correlations decay with distance. The smoothness of the RBF kernel implies that realizations from a GP with this kernel are infinitely differentiable.

\paragraph{Matérn Class of Kernels}
The Matérn kernel family introduces a smoothness parameter $\nu$ that explicitly governs the differentiability of sample functions. For inputs $\bm{x}$ and $\bm{x}'$ with distance $d = \|\bm{x} - \bm{x}'\|$, the Matérn kernel is given by:

\begin{equation}
    k(\bm{x},\bm{x}') = \eta^2 \frac{2^{1-\nu}}{\Gamma(\nu)}\ab(\frac{\sqrt{2\nu}\,d}{\ell})^\nu K_\nu\!\ab(\frac{\sqrt{2\nu}\,d}{\ell}),
\end{equation}

where $K_\nu$ is a modified Bessel function of the second kind and $\Gamma(\cdot)$ is the gamma function. Half-integer values of $\nu$ (e.g., $\nu = 1/2$, $3/2$, $5/2$) yield tractable forms and allow one to model functions ranging from relatively rough ($\nu=1/2$ corresponds to the exponential kernel) to very smooth (high $\nu$). For example, when $\nu=3/2$, the kernel simplifies to

\begin{align}
    k(\bm{x},\bm{x}') = \eta^2 \ab(1 + \frac{\sqrt{3} d}{\ell}) \exp \ab( -\frac{\sqrt{3} d}{\ell}).
\end{align}

 By adjusting $\nu$, one can explicitly control the differentiability of the GP realizations. Kernels may also be combined (e.g., summed or multiplied) to capture multiple scales or patterns in the data. Detailed discussions of kernel design and selection can be found in \citet[][Chapter 4]{rasmussen_gaussian_2006} and \citet{duvenaud_automatic_2014}.

It is important to note that while the GP model is nonparametric in the sense that it does not restrict the functional form to a finite-dimensional family, it does depend on hyperparameters (e.g., $\eta$, $\ell$, and $\nu$ in the Matérn kernel). These hyperparameters are typically learned from the data via maximization of the marginal likelihood (see Section~\ref{sec:gp:regression}) or by placing hyperpriors and performing full Bayesian inference. This balance between data fit and model complexity is one of the main strengths of the GP framework \cite[cf.][pp 110-111]{rasmussen_gaussian_2006}.

\section{Bayesian Inference in Gaussian Processes}\label{sec:gp:bayes}

A Gaussian Process is fundamentally a \textit{Bayesian} model over functions. In typical parametric Bayesian inference, we write

\begin{equation}\label{eq:theory:gp:bayes_rule}
    \underbrace{p(\bm{\theta} \mid \bm{y})}_{\text{posterior}}
    \;=\;
    \underbrace{p(\bm{y} \mid \bm{\theta})}_{\text{likelihood}}
    \,\times\,
    \underbrace{p(\bm{\theta})}_{\text{prior}}
    \,\bigg/\, p(\bm{y}),
\end{equation}

where \(\bm{\theta}\) is a finite-dimensional parameter vector, \(p(\bm{y} \mid \bm{\theta})\) is the likelihood, and \(p(\bm{\theta})\) is the prior. In the GP setting, our “parameter” is the entire function \(f(\cdot)\). Hence the prior is 
\[
  p\bigl(f(\cdot)\bigr) \;=\; \GP\bigl(m(\bm{x}), k(\bm{x},\bm{x}')\bigr),
\]
a distribution over functions, and the likelihood is
\[
  p(\bm{y} \,\mid\, f) \;=\; \prod_{i=1}^N p\bigl(y_i \,\mid\, f(\bm{x}_i)\bigr).
\]
For instance, if our observations are noisy versions of \(f\), say \(y_i = f(\bm{x}_i) + \epsilon_i\) with \(\epsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\text{noise}}^2)\), then
\[
  p(y_i \mid f(\bm{x}_i)) \;=\; \mathcal{N}\bigl(y_i \,\mid\, f(\bm{x}_i), \sigma_{\text{noise}}^2\bigr),
\]
and the full likelihood is a product of Gaussians.

Combining prior and likelihood via Bayes' rule (Equation~\eqref{eq:theory:gp:bayes_rule}) gives the \emph{posterior} distribution over $f$. Unlike a finite-dimensional case, the posterior is now a Gaussian distribution \emph{over functions} (restricted to the finite set of observed points, it becomes a multivariate normal). This is the core mechanism by which GPs learn from data: the likelihood function encapsulates how plausible the observed data are, given a candidate function $f$, and the prior encodes assumptions about smoothness or correlation. The result is a posterior over function space that reflects both prior knowledge and the empirical evidence.


\section{Gaussian Process Regression}\label{sec:gp:regression}

Gaussian process regression (GPR) provides a principled Bayesian framework for function approximation and prediction. Suppose we have $N$ noisy observations $\ab \{ \bm{x}_i, y_i \}_{i=1}^N$ modeled as

\begin{align}\label{eq:theory:gp:generating_process}
    y_i = f(\bm{x}_i) + \epsilon_i, \quad \epsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_{\text{noise}}^2).
\end{align}

Given the GP prior $f(\bm{x}) \sim \GP(0,k(\bm{x},\bm{x}'))$, the joint distribution of the training outputs $\bm{y}=(y_1,\dots,y_N)$ and a test function value $f_*$ at $\bm{x}_*$ is Gaussian:

\begin{equation}
    \begin{pmatrix}
        \bm{y} \\
        f_*
    \end{pmatrix}
    \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(
    \begin{pmatrix}
        \bm{0} \\
        0
    \end{pmatrix},
    \begin{pmatrix}
        \bm{K} + \sigma_{\text{noise}}^2 I & \bm{k}_* \\
        \bm{k}_*^T & k(\bm{x}_*,\bm{x}_*)
    \end{pmatrix}
    \right),
\end{equation}

where $\bm{K}$ is the kernel matrix with $K_{ij} = k(\bm{x}_i, \bm{x}_j)$, and $\bm{k}_*$ is the vector of covariances between the test point and the training points.

Conditioning on the observed data yields closed-form expressions for the predictive mean and variance\footnote{The derivation of these expressions is a standard result in GP regression; see e.g. \citet[][Section~2.3]{bishop_pattern_2006} for a detailed treatment.}:

\begin{subequations}\label{eq:theory:gp:prediction}
    \begin{align}
        \mathbb{E}[f_* | \bm{X},\bm{y},\bm{x}_*] &= \bm{k}_*^T(\bm{K}+\sigma_{\text{noise}}^2 I)^{-1}\bm{y}, \\
        \mathrm{Var}[f_* | \bm{X},\bm{y},\bm{x}_*] &= k(\bm{x}_*,\bm{x}_*) - \bm{k}_*^T(\bm{K}+\sigma_{\text{noise}}^2 I)^{-1}\bm{k}_*.
    \end{align}
\end{subequations} 

The predictive distribution is Gaussian, providing not only a point estimate but also uncertainty quantification. The ability to directly obtain these predictive variances is a hallmark of GPR, making it especially valuable in application where understanding uncertainty is as important as the prediction itself.

An attractive feature of GP regression is that the latent function $f$ can be integrated out to yield the marginal likelihood:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:theory:gp:marginal_likelihood}
    p(\bm{y}|\bm{X}, \bm{\theta}) = \int p(\bm{y}| f) \, p(f | \bm{X}, \bm{\theta}) \, \dd f.
\end{equation}

For a Gaussian likelihood, this integral can be computed in closed form, yielding:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:theory:gp:marginal_likelihood_gaussian}
    \log p(\bm{y}|\bm{X}) = -\frac{1}{2}\bm{y}^T\!\ab (\bm{K}+\sigma_{\text{noise}}^2I)^{-1}\bm{y} - \frac{1}{2}\log \ab |\bm{K}+\sigma_{\text{noise}}^2I | - \frac{N}{2}\log(2\pi).
\end{equation}

Maximizing the log marginal likelihood with respect to the hyperparameters $\bm{\theta} = (\eta, \ell)$ provides a natural mechanism for model selection. In fully Bayesian treatments, one might instead place a hyperprior $p(\bm{\theta})$ and compute

\begin{equation}
    p(\bm{y}|\bm{X}) = \int p(\bm{y}|\bm{X}, \bm{\theta}) \, p(\bm{\theta}) \, \dd \bm{\theta},
\end{equation}

although this integral is typically intractable. As a consequence, gradient-based optimization methods, expectation-maximization algorithms, or cross-validation are commonly used to tune hyperparameters \cite[cf.][Chapter 5]{rasmussen_gaussian_2006}.


\paragraph{Scalability Challenges and Approximations} The inversion of the $N \times N$ matrix $\bm{K} + \sigma_{\text{noise}}$ in Equation~\eqref{eq:theory:gp:prediction} incurs $\mathcal{O}(N^3)$ computational cost and $\mathcal{O}(N^2)$ memory, which limits the direct application of GPs to large datasets. To address this, sparse approximation techniques introduce a set of inducing points, enabling a low-rank approximation of the full covariance matrix that is computationally more efficient to invert \cite[e.g.][]{quinonero-candela_unifying_2005,wilkinson_sparse_2021}.

\section{Beyond Gaussian Likelihoods: Generalized Gaussian Process Models}\label{sec:gp:generalized}

The Gaussian framework described so far assumes that observations are generated from a Gaussian distribution (Equation~\eqref{eq:theory:gp:generating_process}) centered on the underlying function. While this assumption leads to elegant analytical solutions (Equation~\eqref{eq:theory:gp:prediction}), it is often restrictive. Many real-world phenomena produce data that cannot be adequately captured by Gaussian noise models. For instance, environmental count data (e.g., number of species sightings, quantity of beach litter) often follow a Poisson or Negative Binomial distribution, binary observations (e.g., presence/absence data) follow a Bernoulli likelihood, and extreme events or threshold exceedances may require other specialized distributions. In such cases, it is necessary to generalize GP methods to accommodate these non-Gaussian likelihood functions, resulting in what are commonly referred to as \emph{generalized Gaussian process models} (GGPMs) \cite{chan_generalized_2011}. In essence, we still assume a Gaussian process prior on a latent function $f$, but we allow the observations $y_i$ to be generated via a non-Gaussian distribution whose parameters are controlled by $f$.

The key building block of a GGPM is a deterministic \emph{link function} $g$. One posits that each observation $y_i$ is drawn from some family of distributions, with its parameters given by $g(f(\bm{x}_i))$. Symbolically,

\begin{equation*}
    y_i \;|\; f(\bm{x}_i) \sim p( \cdot  \; | \; g(f(\bm{x}_i))).
\end{equation*}

For example, if $y_i$ are counts, one might use a Poisson likelihood whose "rate" or intensity parameter is $\lambda_i = \exp\ab(f(\bm{x}_i))$ \cite[cf.][Section~6.5]{diggle_statistical_2013}. In that setup,

\begin{align}
    p(y_i \; | \; f(\bm{x}_i)) &= \text{Poisson}\ab( y_i \; | \; \exp(f(\bm{x}_i)) ).
\end{align}

The function $\exp (\cdot)$ then acts as the link, ensuring that the Poisson rate parameter $\lambda_i$ remains positive. It is precisely this model, where the logarithm of the rate (or intensity function) is drawn from a Gaussian process, that is known as a \emph{log-Gaussian Cox process} (LGCP) \cite{moller_log_1998}. 

Conceptually, we still treat $f$ as a random function taking values in an infinite-dimensional function space, but we no longer assume that $y_i$ are normally distributed about $f(\bm{x}_i)$. Instead, we feed $f(\bm{x}_i)$ into a suitable link function that controls the non-Gaussian distribution for $y_i$. 

Once we allow a non-Gaussian likelihood, the posterior distribution over the latent function values

\begin{equation}
    p(f \; | \; \bm{y}, \bm{X}) = \frac{p ( \bm{y} \; | \; f ) \; p(f \; | \; \bm{X})}{p( \bm{y} \; | \;\bm{X})}
\end{equation}

loses its convenient Gaussian form. Indeed, for a generic link function $g$, there is no closed-form expression for the posterior or for the predictive distributions. As a result, one typically resorts to approximation techniques such as Laplace approximation \cite[cf.][Section~6.4.6]{bishop_pattern_2006}, variational inference (VI) \cite[cf.][Section~10.1]{bishop_pattern_2006}, expectation propagation (EP) \cite[cf.][Sections~~10.7]{bishop_pattern_2006} or Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods \cite[cf.][Chapter~6]{bishop_pattern_2006} to perform inference. For derivations and detailed discussions of these approximation schemes in the context of GGPMs, see, e.g., \citet{chan_generalized_2011}.

% Laplace Approximation: A second-order Taylor expansion of the log-posterior around its mode yields a multivariate normal approximation to p(f|y,X).
% Variational Inference (VI): One posits a tractable family of approximations (e.g. Gaussians with certain covariance structure) and optimizes a lower bound to the log marginal likelihood.
% Expectation Propagation (EP): The posterior is iteratively approximated by refining local Gaussian "site approximations" for each observation yi.
% Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC): One constructs a stochastic process (e.g.Metropolis--Hastings, Hamiltonian Monte Carlo) that samples from the exact posterior distribution, albeit at higher computational cost.



\section{Spatial and Spatio-Temporal Gaussian Processes}\label{sec:gp:spatial}

While the fundamental formulation of Gaussian processes focuses on relationships between input-output pairs $(\bm{x},f(\bm{x}))$ without explicit reference to space or time, many environmental applications naturally involve data indexed by both spatial and temporal coordinates. Such data often exhibit complex patterns that evolve over multiple spatial scales and across various temporal horizons. Spatial GPs and their spatio-temporal extensions address these challenges by tailoring covariance functions to encode not only spatial correlations but also temporal dependencies.

In purely spatial settings, the input $\bm{x}$ typically represents a location in a two- or three-dimensional domain. The covariance function $k(\bm{x},\bm{x}')$ then quantifies how function values co-vary with spatial separation $\ab \| \bm{x} - \bm{x}' \|$, often assuming stationarity and isotropy for simplicity. For example, the Matérn kernel is widely used because of its flexibility in modeling a range of smoothness properties. Spatial GPs are applied to interpolate, predict, and quantify uncertainty in environmental variables like temperature, precipitation, or pollutant concentrations \cite[e.g][]{datta_nonseparable_2016,wang_use_2020}. Figure~\ref{fig:theory:lgcp} illustrates an example of a spatial LGCP model.

When modeling processes on large spatial domains, such as the Earth's surface, the choice of distance metric in the covariance function becomes critical. While Euclidean distances are convenient, they are not suitable for spherical domains. The geodesic (or great circle) distance is more natural on a sphere. Many standard kernels (including the Matérn class) are originally defined in Euclidean space and may require nontrivial adaptations for use with great circle distances \cite{jeong_spherical_2017,blake_parametric_2022,borovitskiy_matern_2023}. A common approximation is to use chordal distances, which can be justified when the spatial extent is small relative to the curvature of the Earth. In our case study of macroplastic pollution of European beaches (Chapter~\ref{chapter:beach_litter}), the distances involved are so small relative to Earth's circumference, that using the Chordal distance is an appropriate approximation. 

Many environmental processes, however, do not remain static over time. In the case study presented in Chapter~\ref{chapter:beach_litter}, we will handle the fact of seasonal variations by modeling each season independently. However, for long-term temporal processes, this is not practical and a more elegant solution is a spatio-temporal GP which augments the inputs with time coordinates, $(\bm{s}, t)$, where $\bm{s}$ denotes a spatial location and $t$ a point in time. A straightforward approach is to assume a separable covariance structure:

\begin{align}
    k((\bm{s},t),(\bm{s}',t')) = k_{\text{space}}(\bm{s},\bm{s}')k_{\text{time}}(t,t').
\end{align}

While separability simplifies modeling and inference, it may be too restrictive when spatial correlations vary over time. Non-separable kernels allow for interactions between space and time, offering a more realistic representation of dynamic environmental systems \cite{datta_nonseparable_2016}. For instance, one may define:


\begin{equation}
    k\ab((\bm{s},t),(\bm{s}',t')) = \eta^2 \exp\!\ab(-\frac{\|\bm{s}-\bm{s}'\|^2}{2\ell_s^2} - \frac{|t-t'|}{\ell_t}),
\end{equation}

or more sophisticated constructions can be employed to capture non-stationarity in either domain.


\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:gp:conclusion}

Gaussian processes offer a powerful and flexible nonparametric framework for modeling complex data distributions in both small and large-scale environmental contexts. By placing a prior over functions rather than a fixed set of parameters, GPs allow complexity to emerge naturally from the data and provide a principled Bayesian approach to uncertainty quantification. Their ability to handle various kernels, adapt hyperparameters through marginal likelihood optimization, and incorporate non-Gaussian likelihoods through generalized Gaussian process models makes them exceptionally versatile.

This versatility is especially relevant for the sparse and irregular data scenarios considered in this thesis. In Chapter~\ref{chapter:beach_litter}, these concepts are applied to analyze seasonal variations in macroplastic pollution along European beaches. By employing GP-based LGCP models, we move from raw, fragmentary observations to probabilistic statements about pollution intensity across regions and seasons. This approach not only supports more informed ecological and management decisions but also exemplifies how a robust theoretical framework underpins advanced models for sparse environmental data. 

Overall, Gaussian processes provide a rich toolkit that bridges theoretical elegance with practical utility, making them highly relevant for modern environmental modeling challenges.

% When modeling processes on large spatial domains, such as the Earth's surface, the choice of distance metric becomes critical.
