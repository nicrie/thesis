It was in 2011 that \citet{overpeck_climate_2011} projected the worldwide climate data volume to reach 200 petabytes (PB) by 2024. Today, this estimate appears to have been far too conservative. As of August 2024, the meteorological data archive at the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF)---one of the largest of its kind---already stores around 505 PB of operational and research data, with about 0.4 PB added daily \cite{ecmwf_key_2022}. Similarly, the Sentinel Data Access System, which provides data from the Copernicus Sentinel satellite missions, generated approximately 48 PB in 2023, averaging more than 0.1 PB per day \cite{cipoletta_copernicus_2024}. Yet, it is not just the volume that has grown; the variety of data sources and types has also expanded significantly. Initiatives such as the Copernicus Climate Change Service (C3S) \cite{c3s_copernicus_2024} and the European Space Agency's Climate Change Initiative (CCI) \cite{esa_climate_2024} grant access to a wide array of climate data records, including satellite observations, in situ measurements, and reanalysis products. At the same time, citizen science efforts have begun contributing valuable datasets. The Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF), for example, provides more than 3 billion records of species occurrences \cite{gbif_gbif_2024}. In the realm of marine pollution, volunteers have helped record nearly eight million beach litter items through EMODnet Chemistry since 2001 \cite{molina_jack_emodnet_2019, molina_jack_mediterranean_2023, emodnet_beach_2024}, offering crucial insights into the spatial and temporal patterns of anthropogenic pollution.

This immense and rapidly diversifying data landscape reflects the complexity and interconnectedness of the Earth system's physical, chemical, and biological processes. Our challenge lies in identifying meaningful patterns from these data, which span a vast range of spatial and temporal scales---from seconds to centuries and from millimeters to thousands of kilometers~\cite{dickey_role_2001}. In some cases, like climate model outputs, reanalysis products, or satellite imagery, data are dense, regularly sampled, and presented on uniform grids, which simplifies certain analytical tasks. In other scenarios, we must grapple with sparse, irregular measurements, such as those collected through citizen science campaigns or sporadic in situ surveys. While dense datasets demand strategies for condensing complex, massive information into interpretable patterns, sparse datasets require robust methods to glean reliable inferences despite limited and uneven samples. In essence, we must develop and apply advanced statistical and computational methods capable of handling both extremes to extract meaningful knowledge from our increasingly data-rich yet unevenly sampled Earth system.

However, these sophisticated analytical techniques are not always readily accessible. Many advanced statistical and machine learning methods are computationally expensive and require deep theoretical expertise. This can be daunting for domain scientists who want to focus on research questions rather than coding intricacies or method implementation. Free and open-source software (FOSS) can mitigate these challenges by democratizing access to cutting-edge methods, reducing disparities in software availability, and facilitating scientific innovation \cite{hoe_breaking_2006, sonnenburg_need_2007}. Ideally, researchers should be able to concentrate on their scientific objectives---whether exploring a dataset without a predefined hypothesis or testing a specific theory---without being held back by technical hurdles.

From a meta-scientific perspective, the data explosion and the need to extract meaningful patterns has also influenced the practice of science itself \cite{voit_perspective_2019}. Traditionally, hypothesis-driven research has guided the scientific method\footnote{From a modern perspective, it may seem surprising, but during the 17th and 18th centuries---an era of significant scientific progress sparked by the Enlightenment---inductive approaches were widely embraced. These methods involved deriving general principles or laws directly from empirical evidence, rather than from preconceived hypotheses. As \citet{elliott_conceptions_2016} notes, influential thinkers like Francis Bacon and Isaac Newton cautioned that bold conjectures could mislead investigators, advocating instead for careful induction from observed data. For a concise yet insightful overview of the evolution of the scientific method, see \citet[e.g.][]{voit_perspective_2019}.}: formulating a hypothesis, designing experiments, and analyzing results to confirm or refute initial assumptions. This has served as the gold standard for centuries. Yet with the rise in data volumes, data-driven and exploratory approaches have gained prominence, often complementing rather than replacing hypothesis-driven deduction\footnote{\citet{anderson_end_2008}'s bold assertion that the "data deluge makes the scientific method obsolete" appears hyperbolic and has yet to materialize. Nevertheless, it remains intriguing to consider how artificial intelligence (AI), with its potential to autonomously undertake scientific research, may reshape the scientific method in the future \cite{li_paradigm_2024,swanson_virtual_2024}.} \cite{kell_here_2004,glass_critique_2010,voit_perspective_2019}. In this thesis, we employ both paradigms: explorative approaches to uncover hidden structures in dense datasets, and hypothesis-driven frameworks to investigate specific questions in sparse data scenarios.

Although \citet{overpeck_climate_2011} underestimated the sheer magnitude of data growth, their conclusion remains prescient. As the authors rightly noted, the challenge is not merely about storing these data, but about how to analyze them effectively while understanding their uncertainties. Addressing this challenge lies at the heart of this thesis. By developing and applying advanced dimensionality reduction and machine learning techniques, we aim to identify meaningful patterns in both dense and sparse environmental data regimes, paving the way for deeper insights and more informed decision-making in the study of our dynamic Earth system.
